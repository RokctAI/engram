So,what can I DO about it?
(per Klontz)
Avoidance Focus Status Vigilant
Whether you are experiencing financial stress or have negative associations with money or the wealthy, money avoidance can negatively impact your
financial health. Below are some tips that can assist you in challenging these beliefs:
1. Create a Ritual Around Becoming Financially Informed. The best cure for anxiety around money is exposure. Select a time and a place to review your
financial situation. Examples: a weekly meeting with your business partner, a monthly meeting with your spouse, or a quarterly meeting with your
financial advisor.
2. Do Well By Doing Good. Generate a list of ideas regarding how having more money can be good for you and/or beneficial for the world (e.g.,
philanthropic pursuits). Share the list with a close friend or confidante.
3. Identify A Money Mentor. There are many examples of wealthy people who fit the negative stereotype. However, there are many examples of
wealthy people who are helping the poor, curing disease, and doing great things in the world. Whether it is a celebrity or someone in your circle, find a
money mentor using their wealth to make the world a better place. Strive to leverage your positive impact in the world by acquiring the economic
means to do so.
4. Create a New Money Mantra. Create a new money mantra, telling yourself why it is okay for you to have money. For example, your mantra could be:
“The more money I have, the more good I can do in the world.” Keep your new money mantra near where you pay your bills or interact most with your
personal finances, and read it often, for example, in your phone, your wallet or by your computer.
5. Set Budgeting Goals and Reward Yourself! Set achievable budgeting goals, and increase/stretch your goals over time. This way, you do not deprive
yourself, nor will you overspend. Keep track of your goals and reward yourself when you reach them.

So,what can I DO about it?
(per Klontz)
Avoidance Focus Status Vigilant
We live in a society that puts money on a pedestal. As a result, Money Focused beliefs are common, and even when they are destructive, they are often
supported and revered. It can be difficult to swim against the tide, but tempering the impulse to attach our happiness to our income, net worth, and
possessions is good for us, our relationships, and our pocketbooks.
1. Escape Buyer’s Remorse: Buying new things can be exciting and fun, but shortly after the purchase, the thrill goes away. If you find yourself regretting
purchases after you make them, a great practice is to put time between your impulse to buy something and pulling the trigger on that purchase.
Journal or ask yourself questions about your intention behind purchasing a certain item. For example, ask yourself: “Is this something my family or I
need? Am I so tired from working so hard that I feel I deserve a treat? How does this purchase fit with my values and goals?"
After reflecting on these questions, you may still end up making the purchase, but you will find value in slowing down your thoughts before taking
action.
2. Make Time for Yourself and The People You Love: Yes, having lots of money is great, but what will you do with it if there is no one around with whom
you can share it? Just like with purchases, achieving wealth can quickly lose its luster if it’s not attached to our values and goals. For most people, this
involves spending time doing things we love with the people we love.
When we attach money to our happiness, it can be difficult to curb our impulse to work longer and harder because we are rewarded with more
money when we do so. However, keeping our priorities in mind can help us maintain a healthy work-life balance.
Set aside specific times throughout your week to connect with loved ones. For example, let them know, as a reminder to yourself and as a reminder to
them, that they are special to you, that you are reserving specific evenings to be with them and to be fully present. It will recharge you and your
relationships and is much more likely to bring you happiness than money alone.
3. Give. It feels great to give to others, whether to your favorite charity or as a demonstration of love and support to friends and family members.
However, just like any expense, giving should be budgeted, tracked, and done with intention. It’s not healthy to give beyond one’s means, to support
financial dependence on others, or to give out of a sense of guilt. Time spent supporting people and causes you love can be just as valuable as a
financial gesture.

So,what can I DO about it?
(per Klontz)
Avoidance Focus Status Vigilant
We live in a culture that associates financial status with social standing. It is normal to desire the “latest and greatest,” toys and we all want to be
successful. These thoughts and feelings are normal, but they can lead to detrimental financial outcomes when left unexamined. You are not your net
worth? it is a part of you, of course, but it need not define you. Here are some tips to keep money status scripts in check:
1. Slow Down. Before making a purchase, slow your thought process and ask yourself the following questions: 1) Why am I buying this item? 2) What
does buying this item do for me emotionally? 3) How long is that feeling likely to last? 4) Can I afford this? 5) How will I pay for this item? 6) How will I
feel about this purchase next week? When you answer these questions, look for flaws in your logic, such as “buying this item will make me appear
impressive or cool.” Try to feel the emotions you might experience as you make the purchase: excitement, disappointment, stress, anxiety, shame, or
fulfillment? How does naming your emotion impact your desire to continue with the purchase? Is there a better way to meet this emotional need?
2. Make Time. Determine a time and a place to discuss your monthly spending with a loved one or trusted advisor. This can be over dinner, on the second
Tuesday of every month, or some other time that works for you. The goal is to get your financial situation out into the open and focus on it consciously
and purposefully.
3. Strive to be Emotionally, Physically, and Financially Healthy. Some people who endorse money status beliefs are quite financially successful. Their
drive to secure their social status and gain access to the finer things in life have been successful. However, sometimes a drive for monetary success is
accompanied by workaholism, which can harm marriages, relationships with children, and physical and psychological well-being. Make sure to take
time for what matters most. Financial success without health and fulfilling relationships is worthless.

So,what can I DO about it?
(per Klontz)
Avoidance Focus Status Vigilant
Having financial comfort and security is critically important. However, excessive anxiety and wariness around money can keep you up at night and be bad
for your health. The tips below are designed to help you responsibly enjoy your resources. Your advisor can help you set up a financial plan to balance out
your need for security with your need to enjoy what you have worked so hard to build.
1. Have Fun! Set a “fun money” budget. Use your skills as a financial analyst and saver to put together a fun activity for yourself and/or your family.
Consider taking a long vacation or buying yourself a new toy, even if it makes you anxious at first. Creating space in your budget to enjoy your hard work
can pay dividends in feelings of gratitude and joy and deeper connections with those you love most.
2. You Can’t Take it With You. Spend some money on something ridiculous. Gulp. This is a difficult assignment for the money vigilant. But what’s the
point of a lifetime spent in frugality, deprivation, and saving if you never allow yourself or your family to enjoy it? The money vigilant have mastered the
habit of saving after decades of hard work and practice. In this same way, the money vigilant often need to practice shifting to spending once they have
reached their financial goals. Whether it’s that trip you fantasized about taking or a visit to the spa for a massage, practice spending money on something
ridiculous to break the ice.
3. Have a Trusted Partner or Advisor. Check-in with a trusted advisor or friend. It can be difficult to talk about money with other people, and in many
instances, a little secrecy is even a good thing. Conversely, discussing your financial situation with someone who can provide insight and help you broaden
your perspective can be extremely valuable. While Money Vigilance is excellent in terms of one’s ability to save and live responsibly, don’t let your
healthy vigilance turn into anxiety or a tendency to hoard out of fear.
If watching over your financial situation takes up more than an hour a week, keeps you up at night, or gets in the way of other activities you want to do, it
may be time for a change. You can set an intention to actively spend less time worrying about your financial status. For example, if you spend an hour a
day reviewing your portfolio, consider 30 minutes each day or an hour every other day. Scale back gradually and make a mental note or write on a sticky
note in a visible place that you are exactly where you need to be and are on track to meet your financial goals.
