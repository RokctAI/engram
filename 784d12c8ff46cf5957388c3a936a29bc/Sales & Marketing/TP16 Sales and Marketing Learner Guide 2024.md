1. MARKETING: AN OVERVIEW
How is marketing different from sales?
Many people think that sales and marketing are one concept, so intertwined that they cannot easily
be separated. In truth, they are two related but very different disciplines, with a common end goal –
organizational growth, sustainability and success.
In simple terms we use marketing to find, communicate with, inspire and attract potential supporters,
funders, investors, partners and customers. We then apply our sales magic to seal the deal!
Some definitions you should know:
Marketing is the means by which an organization communicates to, connects with, and engages its
target audience to convey the value of and ultimately sell its products and services. Since the
emergence of digital media, in particular social media and technology innovations, it has increasingly
become more about companies building deeper, more meaningful and lasting relationships with the
people that they want to support or buy their products and services.
Some marketing terms
A target market is a group of customers that the business has decided to target with its marketing
efforts and ultimately its products. A well-defined target market is the first element to an effective sales
and marketing strategy.
Inbound marketing refers to marketing activities that bring visitors in, rather than marketers having to
go out to get prospects’ attention. Inbound marketing earns the attention of customers, makes the
company easy to be found, and draws customers to a contact point such as a website by producing
interesting content. (BEES TO HONEY)
Outbound marketing involves all the proactive marketing steps we undertake to communicate with
our target market/s, to create awareness of our business and products, and to encourage customer
loyalty and repeat business. It includes things like advertising, media and PR, customer surveys, etc.
A brand is the identity of a specific product, service, or business. A brand can take many forms,
including a name, sign, symbol, colour combination or slogan. Brands should be seen as more than
just a logo however. They represent the sum of all valuable qualities of a product or company to the
consumer.
The marketing mix refers to the set of actions, or tactics, that a company uses to promote its brand or
product in the market. It can involve direct marketing actions, e-marketing, social media, brand
activations, advertising campaigns and a whole host of other marketing activities.

Ask a few people to name a logo they like.

around.” Seth Godin.

2. UNDERSTANDING THE MARKET
Typically, when starting a business most people are tempted to try to sell to everyone – believing that
by including the whole world as a possible customer they will increase the chances of success.
Interestingly, the opposite is in fact usually true, and unless you are selling mass consumer goods such as
water or electricity, a business that identifies & focuses on a specific target market is more, not less
likely to thrive.
Before you decide about the what, where, why and how of your marketing strategy and approach, you
need to determine the WHO.
Who are the people, clients, groups, end users or organisations that are most likely to support your
products & services? This could be the end users, or the partners you need to reach them.
The starting point for any SE that wants to improve both its sales and marketing success, is to decide
who your target market is. Why do you think this is the case
• If you know who you are looking for, you can determine where to find them
• If you know who you are talking to, you can determine what you should be saying
• If you know who you are serving, you can ensure that you meet their
needs Knowing your target market is the starting point – everything else
stems from there:

2.1 The Value Chain
The Value Chain is the journey your product or service takes before it ends up in the hands of the end
user. Other players (besides you and the end user) in the Value Chain could include your channel
partners - wholesalers, retailers, agents, sector specialists, NPOs, Funders etc.
Example – Typical Production Value Chain
Example – Typical Service Value Chain
The reason these other players are so important is that often without them you will not be able to access
certain markets – for example, an international NGO might be able to get your products into a country
they are active in, or a health product wholesaler might be able to get your products onto the shelves at
Spar, or a property developer might be able to help your environmental consultancy by marketing your
services to their clients on a wholesale basis.
Your job is to know what value chain players you need, and to make them aware of your products and
services and the value you can bring to their businesses.
People often criticise the ‘Middle Man’, but the truth is that the Value Chain needs the various links, with
each person making their own profit, in order to work. That said, if there are too many ‘links’ in the chain
then your product or service can end up being too expensive when it reaches the end user, leading to
failure. So, a streamlined, efficient and effective Value Chain is always best.

2.2 Market positioning
Once you have decided who your primary target market/s are, your next step is to position yourself in their
minds. What is the perception of your company and your brand that you want to create in relation to
competing brands or businesses? This is known as market positioning.
Market positioning refers to the process of establishing the image or identity of your brand, so that
your target market perceives it in a certain way.
Some examples:
• A car maker may position itself as an eco-friendly alternative to the rest e.g., Tesla
• A battery maker may position its batteries as the most reliable and long-lasting e.g., Duracell
• A fast-food restaurant chain may position itself as a provider of healthy and nutritious meals e.g.,
Kauai
• A coffee company may position itself as the place to go for sustainable, socially impactful coffee
e.g. I Love Coffee
• A retailer might position itself as the best place to buy clothing at low prices e.g., Mr Price
• A computer company may position itself as offering hip, innovative and user-friendly
technology products e.g., Apple

2.3 Leveraging your story
Establishing your market positioning starts with the creation of a fundamental core brand message - a
compact statement that declares why the brand matters, what it stands for and how it is stands
apart from competitors. Often, this is based on the Vision and Mission of your business.
Your marketing message is what grabs your prospect’s attention, tells them how you can solve their
problem, why they should trust you, and why they should choose to do business with you over and above
all other choices they might have. Above all else, it makes your target audience sit up and … care.
This core message often needs to resonate with potential funders & partners, over and above the
actual end users or beneficiaries of the products &/or services.
It’s a message that will guide and inform all your other marketing efforts and communications, so you
need to get it right.
Remember, you need to think how you want to be POSITIONED in the minds of your target audience
when you are creating your core message:
• A firm that wants to differentiate itself in the marketplace may formulate a core message or
marketing campaign theme that contrasts itself with the rest of the industry (Avis’ “We try
harder” comes to mind).
• A firm that wants to position itself as more efficient and customer-facing will choose a core
message like Standard Bank’s “Simpler. Better. Faster”
• A firm that wants to position itself as at the forefront of innovation would choose a core
message such as Apple’s “Think Different”.
Crafting messages that are concise, easy to understand and tell a coherent story can be a lot trickier
than you think. But once you’ve found your voice, you’ll discover that people suddenly do a lot more
listening!
So, how do YOU want to be positioned?

3. PUTTING IT INTO PRACTICE
We have spent some time distilling what it is that makes you special above. Now we want to help you put
this into practice, so let’s start with a story….
Imagine that you live deep in a thick, impenetrable forest. While experimenting with some of the herbs
you find growing in the forest, you develop the most amazing product in the world – an anti-aging cream
that reverses the ravages of getting old – after a few simple applications, this wonder cream makes the
user look at least ten years younger, with no side –effects!!
Clearly, there will be massive demand for such a product all over the world.
Problem is, you are hidden away from civilization deep in this forest. Nobody knows where you are,
how to find you or anything about you, your brand, your company, or your product. You are sitting on
a potential goldmine, but unless you can spread the word, it is worthless. So what do you need to
do?
This is where the marketing function begins …
3.1 Identify your target market - Activity
When it comes to marketing (and sales!), half the battle is knowing who is most likely to support your
business – in other words your target market, as discussed above.
The reason you need to identify a target market is because it makes finding and communicating with your
potential customers, as well as designing, pricing, distributing, promoting, positioning and improving
your product, service or idea easier, more successful, and more cost-effective.
Your target market analysis should answer the following questions at the very least:
• Is there a gap in the market for my offering? Explain.
• What kinds of customers want my product or service? (age, gender, buying habits)
• What sort of products or services do they want?

Once your initial analysis is complete, it’s time to dig a bit deeper. You will find that you have different
groups of customers and to ensure your marketing efforts are speaking to all of them, it is worth spending
the time to segment them and develop customer personas.
Brands often use segmentation to help understand and target an audience. This means taking into
consideration general details about the average customer, such as their demographic, gender, socio-
economic status and so on. This information can then be used to develop customer personas.
Personas can be one of the most effective ways of bringing this customer segmentation to life.
So, you may be wondering, what exactly are customer personas, and why are they so important?
A customer persona (or buyer persona/user persona) is a fictional character that you make up to
represent your ideal customer. It’s usually based on market research and any data you’ve collected
from your existing customers.
When creating your buyer or customer persona you should include:
• Customer demographics
• Their age, gender and occupation
• Their behaviour patterns
• Their pain points
• Your customer’s motivations and goals
• The value you provide to them
Customer personas are a big help when it comes to building up a strong insight into what your
customers are like. A detailed persona will allow you to focus on what parts of your business appeal to
your customers. It will help you align your services with what your customers want to see.
With strong customer personas at your fingertips, you will be able to attract more valuable visitors to your
social media tribe and website that will more likely convert and become a paying client for your
business.

3.3 Build a pathway to your business
Having created some awareness, the next thing you need to do is make it easy for people to find you and
more importantly, your product. Clearly, no-one will come and buy your product if it is impossible to get
through the thick forest, even if they really want it. So, your job is to build a pathway that makes it easy
for them.
In our forest context, this would mean getting a bulldozer/machete and clearing a path so that
people could easily travel to your door. Maybe you will need to build a helicopter landing pad or
develop a chartered transport solution to make it easy for your potential customers to reach you.
In traditional marketing, what does this mean? It means taking steps to make accessing you, your
products and services, an easy, pleasant, and hassle-free experience for potential customers. It would
involve things like
• Aligning with statutory bodies and relevant industry associations so they have your details and can
direct interested parties to you e.g. Institute of Architects, Master Builder’s Association,
agricultural bodies, etc.
• Effective and visible signage – for example if you have a shop or a showroom/office, or on
your company vehicle, if you have one.
• Being easily contactable and keeping details up to date (email, cellphone).
• Making sure you appear in any relevant industry publications, such as newsletters, brochures
and magazines.
• Spreading the news by presenting your product through more than one outlet, in more than one
area – this may mean doing trade shows, exhibitions, etc.
• Registering on any relevant procurement databases.
• A professional online presence, including an up-to-date website, and social media platforms,

3.5 Create customers for life
A final element of marketing is about encouraging customer loyalty. Ideally, we all want customers who
not only come back and buy from us time and again, but also speak positively of us to others. This is
known as ‘word-of- mouth’ marketing, and it is one of the most powerful forms of marketing around.
In our forest home, we can encourage customer loyalty by making sure every visitor has a memorable
experience and gets more value than they were expecting. Perhaps we can offer free tea or coffee to
them after their long journey to find us, or a small gift to take away to remind them of their visit. Maybe
we take each of them on a tour of the forest and surrounds, showing them the process of how we came
to develop such an amazing product. Free samples and generosity are also never a bad strategy.
In a traditional context, businesses encourage customer loyalty by:
• listening to their customers
• always keeping their promises and being willing to help
• inspiring confidence (good product knowledge)
• treating every customer as an individual
• making it easy for customers to do business with them
• ensuring all physical aspects of the company, as well as its products and services, give a
favourable impression
• offering great after-sales service, generous warranties and peace of mind
• genuinely going the extra mile
• always looking to solve problems

4. Telling your business story
There was a time when a company’s “About Us” page did not matter quite so much: A short bio, a
mission statement and you are done. These days, consumers care more and more about buying locally,
supporting independent businesses, and owning products that are made sustainably and responsibly.
They want to know the story of what they are buying, who is selling it and what causes it may support. In
a business landscape where success hinges on establishing a personal connection with customers and
investors, the “About Us” page has become prime real estate.
“A lot of business owners fall in love with their own product and forget that other people need to be
romanced by a story. A brand should make you feel something when you say the name. Without context,
it’s just stuff.”
These days, having that “About Us” page that paints a complete picture is more important than ever. In
tough economic times when consumers have fewer discretionary rands, they want to feel good spending
that money. Whether the story is about sustainability, buying local or being an underdog, we want to feel
there’s a person behind the company that we’re buying into, and that there’s a sense of purpose we can
relate to.
Good stories open doors. Without one, your business may not get the attention it deserves.
Before crafting your story, think about the following: What is your goal? Make sure you know exactly how
you want your audience to act after hearing your story. Do you want them to buy your product or
service? Join the company? Invest? Stories can power you toward a goal only if the message is crystal
clear.
Let’s look at the three basic components of telling your business story in an effective and
compelling way. The three basic storytelling questions:
• Who Are You?
• What Do You Do?
• Why Do You Do It?
These may seem obvious, but the importance of answering all three is still often overlooked. Let’s
take a look at each of these questions and how to answer them effectively.
Who Are You?
There are several aspects to this:
• Your history and company culture.
• Your people: names, faces, and stories – not just those of the founder(s), but everyone who
works with you (up to a point, of course).
• Your vision, philosophy, and values. What would the world look like if it were up to you?
• Your mission and objectives. Now we know what you want the world to look like; what is your
commitment to making it head in that direction?

5. BRANDING BASICS
A brand is the identity of a specific product, service, or business. A brand can take many forms, including
a name, sign, symbol, colour combination or slogan. However, brands should be seen as much more
than just a logo – they represent the sum of all valuable qualities of a product/service/company to the
consumer.
Simply put, your brand is your promise to your customer. It tells them what they can expect from your
products and services, and it differentiates your offering from your competitors’.
Are you the innovative maverick in your industry? Or the experienced, reliable and steady option? Is
your product the high-cost, high-quality option, or the low-cost, bargain option?
REMEMBER: you can’t be all things to all people. Who you are should be based to some extent on
who your target customers want and need you to be.
Brand Equity
Consistent, strategic branding leads to strong brand equity, which means the added value brought to
your company’s products or services that allows you to charge more for your brand than what identical,
unbranded products command. The most obvious example of this is Coke vs. a generic soda. Because
Coca-Cola has built a powerful brand equity, it can charge more for its product - and customers will
pay that higher price.
The added value of brand equity frequently comes in the form of perceived quality or emotional attachment. For
example, Nike associates its products with star athletes, hoping customers will transfer the emotional
attachment they feel for the athlete to the product. For Nike, it’s not just the shoe’s features that sell the shoe –
it’s the brand equity as well.
What is a Brand Promise?
A brand promise is an extension of your company’s positioning – the work we did at the beginning of
the day. If you think of positioning as the fertile ground that allows a brand to germinate, grow and
thrive, the brand promise is a brand’s fruit — it’s the tangible benefit that makes a product or service
desirable.
A few years ago, FedEx declared that it was the only choice “when it
absolutely, positively has to get there overnight” — an overt promise
that still resonates today.
So why would a business risk everything on a brand promise?
After all, wouldn’t it be safer to make no promises at all to
clients?
In fact, the opportunity cost of hiding in the shadows is
enormous. If a business is confident in its abilities and has
developed a compelling and consistent customer experience,
it can more easily differentiate itself and assume a market
leadership position. Clients will choose these businesses more
often because they appear to offer more value.

9. INTERPERSONAL SELLING
Have you heard the phrase “Telling is not selling”? Good salespeople understand that selling is about
understanding the customer – asking questions and listening carefully to the answers.
There are some techniques we can master to help us be better salespeople. The most important of these,
is learning how to LISTEN.
9.1 Open ended questions
These are questions that do not just have a ‘yes’ or ‘no’ answer but require some more comment.
They are very useful in allowing a salesperson to determine the customers’ needs and make
suggestions to satisfy those needs.
Examples of open-ended questions in a sales environment could include:
- What is the most important outcome you are looking for in terms of my service or product?
- What timeline do I need to work within?
- Who is the ultimate decision maker in terms of this project?
- Which of my products or services have caught your eye?
- What sort of budget did you have in mind?
- What specific design do you need?
- What size would be preferable?
- What colours would you prefer?
Remember, sales is about asking the right questions, listening to the answers and being guided by the
customers responses.
9.2 Active listening
Active listening is a structured form of listening and responding that focuses the attention on the
speaker. The listener must take care to attend to the speaker fully, and then repeats, in the listeners own
words, what he or she thinks the speaker has said. This enables the speaker to find out whether the listener
really understood. If the listener did not, the speaker can explain some more.
Why is it important?
• First, it forces people to listen attentively to others.
• Second, it avoids misunderstandings, as people have to confirm that they do really understand
what another person has said.
• Third, it tends to open people up, to get them to say more.

sabfoundationtholoana@fetola.co.za

An example of active listening would be to say, “So am I correct in saying that you are looking for a
compact home solar system, preferably one that can power your geyser and lights, with a budget of
around R3 000?”
Reading the customer
Good salespeople all share a special ability to “read the customer”. (Reading the customer means
understanding what they want even if they haven’t told you!)
It involves looking at their body language, listening to what they say and reading between the lines.
For example, if you are showing a customer a range of your products and he says: “My wife really
likes eco- friendly products”, what is he really saying?
It is surprisingly easy to “read” your customer, once you recognise what to look for.
There are four things to watch:
1. Body language: are they constantly touching a certain product or products?
2. Looking: where are they looking, and at what products?
3. Saying: do they mention a certain service or product more than once?
4. Responding: how do they respond or react to your prompts and suggestions?
The customer is then able to either confirm that you have understood them correctly, or else correct
anything you may have misunderstood, thus minimising the potential for mistakes and
misinterpretations.

10.2 Researching your customer
Once you have identified a potential client, the next step is to find out more about them. This comes in
two parts – first, find out or confirm their name and contact information; and second, assess if they
qualify for your attention.
Step one to researching a client:
1. Use the internet (e.g., websites, Facebook, LinkedIn) to find out the name and contact
information of the position in authority.
2. Call their receptionist to ask for their contact information and any other information about that
person that might be useful to you. For example, are they the person who makes the purchasing
decisions? When is the best time to contact them? Do they have a secretary/ PA and if so, what
is his/her name?
3. Find out if they’re the MAN.
Top tips:
Never combine research of the client with the request for a meeting. This comes across as unprofessional
and will likely result in you being fobbed off to the secretary/PA. Rather make the research call to
gather the information you need, then end the call and prepare yourself properly before calling
back.
Who is the MAN?
MAN is an acronym for:
Money – do they have the money/budget? Are they ultimately responsible for spending it? How
much do they have to spend and when? In large organisations with annual budgets, it is important
to know what this budget cycle is and to tailor your approach accordingly.
Authority – is this person authorised to make the sale and spend the money? Or do they need to get
permission from someone higher up in the company? If this is the case, rather seek out that person
and sell directly to them. Sales to a person too low on the hierarchy are too easily delayed and
deferred and the ‘need to consult my boss’ becomes a barrier. If you do find yourself confronted
with this in the sales meeting, be sure to request that you be the one making the presentation to the
boss – as you have the best product knowledge after all.
Need – there is no point in trying to sell your products/services to someone who is not in need of
them, so be sure to research if this person is in fact a potential client. For example, there is no point
trying to sell baby nappies to a couple without children.

One solution for overcoming this is ‘coming alongside’ the client.
Using the analogy of a boat, heading directly at an oncoming boat can only result in an unpleasant
confrontation, and a collision. For example, if you are of the opinion that the client is wrong, and your
solution is right…and you tell them so!
A better solution is to sidle up next to the client by asking them to tell you what they currently do, to
explain their thinking and current strategy. Then in an appropriate manner praise them for that.
Remember people always prefer to be praised than to be criticised! Now that you are ‘alongside
them’ and have created a safe space where they feel you are ‘on board’ with their thinking, the next
step is to ask them if there are any challenges, problems or concerns with the existing arrangement.
Now they will start to open up, and to share what isn’t working so well. You can safely ask questions and
gently probe them for more information. This is the gap. The problem that you can solve!
11.3 Formulating a solution
A good salesperson is always looking for a win-win situation. That is one where the client is genuinely
satisfied (preferably delighted) with the product and has a price that delivers good value. You, on the
other hand, need to make a profit and be in a position to deliver a product of which you are proud.
For this reason, customer-centred companies looking to find solutions for their customers might consider
the following approach
• Figure out the problem you’re trying to solve for them
Make sure you’re offering to solve the right problem and not trying to provide a fix for
something that isn’t a priority in the eyes of your client. In the above section we have discovered
this by asking simple questions like ‘what’s missing from our product or service that would make it
better?’ This can go a long way towards defining your direction at this stage.
You will have written down all of these needs/problems so that you can confirm your
understanding of them and fit a solution to match.
Now is the time to repeat/reflect back to them the problem and confirm this is what they want to
solve.
• Analyse the problem
Ask how often the problem occurs, how severe it is, what are the causes, and what, if any special
circumstances impact it? Another focus should be on the timeframe of the problem. For how
long has it been occurring? Has it been getting worse with time and, if not, are there factors
that could cause it to do so in the future?
Be sure to assess how important the problem is by asking. For example, don’t assume that price is
the issue when the client is more concerned by reliability or delivery. In fact, never assume
anything; always ask.

• Offer a solution

12. DEALING WITH OBJECTIONS
Getting objections is just part of the sales and negotiation process and should be dealt with as such.
If you have followed the simple steps outlined in the processes above – i.e. qualifying the MAN, asking
questions about their need, asking about common challenges, checking budget cycles, and their price
expectations – you should have been able to make an offer that matches their needs and simply
close the deal.
But if your sales process goes to an objection, here are very simple techniques that will help you.
12.1 An objection is an opportunity to align your needs
Just as we thank customers that bring us negative feedback, we should look at objections as a great
way to really get aligned with their need. The way to do that is to ask questions.
Start by asking questions that will identify where you have a mismatch. Typically, you need to run
back through your notes on their need and reconfirm these:
“Mr Jones, I understand that you were looking for a solution that was guaranteed, could be delivered
in two days and was provided by a local provider. We have met those needs, so what have we
missed? What other concerns or challenges do I need to understand?”
12.2 The four most common sales objections and how to handle them
There are four main objections you’ll hear when you’re trying to make a sale, and they revolve around
budget, authority, need, and timeliness (also known as BANT). Knowing how to recognise these
objections and having a process in place to handle them is key to closing a sale or removing a poor-fit
prospect from your sales queue. We’re going to walk you through each objection and give you tips
for addressing them.
Objection 1: Budget
This is probably the easiest objection to identify: “It’s all about the moolah, baby”. Prospects may say
things like, “I don’t have money for your service”, or “There’s no room in the budget for us to buy your
product.” They may even ask if you can give them a discount. So when a person brings up the issue
of money, how should you respond?
• How to handle the objection
Concerns about the cost of your product can go deeper than the quoted price. It’s up to you to
figure out what the true reasons are for their indecision. Dig deeper to find out why they aren’t happy
with the price. Is it just shock? Or is it that they don’t see the value in your offering for the price that
they have to pay? Getting to the root of the objection is key and it will help you choose the right
language to use to overcome it.
If it really is just shock, you can explain about all the components that make up your product or
service and emphasise how much value they’re receiving for the price they are paying. If the
prospect isn’t convinced of the value, you’ll probably want to explain how much he or she could
save using your product or service. Both conversations are similar, yet slightly different to better

Have a policy never to discount. Discounting reduces the value of your offering and trains the

It is much better to offer more for the same price, for example: “If you take three I can give you

Or simply reduce the total ticket into their budget, for example: “I tell you what, why don’t we
reduce the size of your order so it fits within your budget?”
Objection 2: Authority
In this objection, prospects may try to pawn the decision off on someone else. This is a very common
objection, especially if there are multiple decision-makers within a company. You’ll hear, “I have to speak
with my boss/CEO/ designer/co-worker/mother about this before deciding,” or “My
boss/CEO/designer/co-worker/mother says no thank you.” So what do you do when you’re given the
slip because of someone else?
• How to handle the objection
First: if you qualified that you were speaking to the MAN, this objection should not arise. So ask, “I
understood from our earlier conversation that you had the authority to make this decision, is there
anything that I missed?”
Second: offer to go with the person into that meeting with their superior, after all you know your
product better than anyone. “Great! I would love to present to your superior, when and where do
you need me?”
Third: validate that the objection makes sense. Again repeat your conversation: “I understood from
our conversation that you needed x, y, z and we have met that, is there anything else that I
should understand?”
Lastly, if you fail with the above, thank them and set a date and time for the next meeting so you can
get feedback.
Objection 3: Need
Especially for complex products and services, this is a prevalent objection that sales representatives will
face. You’ll recognise this when someone says something like, “You know, we really don’t need your
product or service”, or “Your product is too hard to use, we’ll just stick to our old ways of doing things and
get by.” This is probably the hardest objection to handle since it revolves around the actual product or
service you’re selling.
• How to handle the objection
As in the above, ideally you should have been checking and confirming the need through the
discussion and providing a solution that meets this stated need. But if you find yourself in this
position, ask questions:
“Mr Nyathi, I understood that you were looking to replace your existing water hoses and
needed one that could be delivered in 14 days. We are able to meet your needs, so what have we
missed? What has changed? What other concerns or challenges do I need to understand?”
Like in Objection 2, you’ll want to first get to the root of the problem. Do they view your entire product
as something worthless, or are there a few features they love? Or, are they just overwhelmed with your

GETTING TO YES – Tips to close the deal
Whether you’re a startup or an established entrepreneur, here are a baker’s dozen of our best
commandments for sealing the deal.
Ask for the sale! The whole purpose of the discussion/meeting is to sell, so ask for the sale.
Remain seated: The saying goes: present the product, service or idea on your feet, but always
negotiate from your seat. Even if your prospect stands up, remain seated. Going from a seating
position to standing up suggests something has changed and allows your prospect to exit and end
the negotiations.
Always present a proposal in writing: People do not believe what they hear, they believe what they
see. Always have a contract available and a writing pad. Anything offered or points of value that are
included should be written down to show buyers what they get when they make a decision with you.
This can be typed up and sent through in a professional format after the meeting.
Communicate clearly: No one will trust a person who cannot communicate clearly and confidently.
Some people practice using recorders and video and then play them back to ensure their
communication is coming across the way they intended.
Make eye contact: This is a discipline instilled only through practice, and you can perfect it by
recording yourself. If you want to be believed, it is vital to make eye contact with your prospect. It
suggests interest in them and confidence in yourself, your products, your services, and in what you
are proposing.
Always carry a pen. All agreements require signatures and that requires ink. Keep a pen available at all
times. In fact, always have a back-up pen, too.
Use humour: Any humour that can make people feel good, inspired or hopeful is always appropriate
during the close. Everyone loves a good story, and people are more likely to make decisions when
they are less serious. You will close more deals if you can get your client to lighten up and laugh.
Ask one more time: Figuring out another way to circle back and reposition negotiations after being
told “no” ultimately will make you a great closer. It is not rude to persist; it is the sign of success and
prosperity. Because you continue to ask in another way for a “yes” after being told “no” does not
mean you did not listen. It only means you are more sold on your view than you are on the other’s
view.
Stay with the buyer: Each time you leave the customer to check on something, it creates doubt and
uncertainty in their mind. It can create undue antagonism in the negotiations, lower perceived
value, and extend the closing time. But keep in mind, this does not mean there is not an appropriate
time to leave a buyer and use an authority for a close, as this can be very powerful as long as it is not
overused.
Always treat prospects like buyers: Regardless of the circumstances: no money, no budget, not the
decision maker – always treat the prospect like they are a buyer. Always survey the prospect for signs
that demonstrate they have bought in the past. The watch, the shirt, the suit, the necklace, the car
they drove, the house they live in, the credit card they use, and others. All are evidence that this
prospect has actually demonstrated the ability and history of closing. Tell yourself, “Every buyer is a
buyer. Treat them as a buyer and they will turn into a buyer.”
Ask for what you want: At some stage you simply need to ask for what you want. You may need to
employ close-ended questions for this part of the process, such as ‘Who shall I make the invoice out
to?’, or ‘If we delivered next week Wednesday would that work for you?’. Other times you will need
to be a bit more subtle, but at some stage you are going to have to ask for what you want.
Be positive: No matter how the buyer responds, keep it light and maintain a can-do attitude
throughout the negotiations. When you go negative due to the buyer being negative there is only one
outcome, and it’s not good. Negativity always succumbs to positivity.
Always smile: This is not just about your attitude, but also your physical manifestation. For the next
week, practice smiling with everyone in every situation you encounter. Do this until you are able to
argue with a smile, disagree with a smile, negotiate, overcome objections and close with a smile.

14. WINNING WITH SALES
14.1 Attributes of successful salespeople
The techniques and methods of sales are teachable to anyone, but there are a few natural skills of
great benefit if you want to be successful. Take time to master these!
A good salesperson, whether you are selling cars, cakes or services, needs the following attributes:
1. Effective communication
Master the ability to speak clearly and confidently. Sales is all about talking to people and getting
them to understand what you are trying to communicate. Don’t mumble and avoid talking too
fast as many of us do when we get a bit nervous. Speak clearly and slowly!
2. Ability to listen
A great salesperson knows when to stop talking and listen. They never cut someone off while they are
talking, because in doing so, they could fail to hear a key element in identifying what that
person’s needs might be. Sales is all about understanding and meeting the customer’s needs, so
active listening is the most important skill in sales. Learn how to listen!
3. Ask great questions
Ask questions that identify the real need or desire of the buyer. Ask the right questions!
4. Be a problem solver
Great salespeople are always solving problems. The ability to identify the buyer’s problem and offer
suggestions to solve the problem through your products or services, generally results in a sale. Provide
solutions to a problem!
5. Be organised
Good salespeople have a keen ability to break things down into smaller steps and organise a plan of
action. They know how to analyse what the goal is and in what order the steps need to be in order to
reach that goal. Organise everything!
6. Be a self-starter and self-finisher
A successful salesperson never needs anyone to tell them when it is time to go to work because
they know that if they do not work, they will not succeed. They are also very persistent and finish what
they start. They achieve their goals, even if they are small ones. Drive your own goals
7. Positive self-image
A “can-do” attitude is very common among good salespeople. They are confident and do not let
difficulties get them down or affect their work. Stay positive!
8. Be well mannered and courteous
The best salespeople are very well mannered. Good manners are a way of showing respect for others.
People are attracted to those that respect them, and mutual respect is fundamental in building
lasting relationships with people … including buyers. This includes punctuality and time-keeping –
being late or missing an appointment is an absolute relationship killer in sales. It shows a lack of
respect for people’s time and is simply not excusable in any shape or form. Be on time!

9. Be naturally persuasive

Appendix 1: THE LANGUAGE OF SALE
B2B Sales
B2B is short for “business to business.” It indicates sales made to other businesses, rather than sales to
individuals. The latter is referred to as “business to consumer” (B2C) sales.
Conversion Rate
The percentage of people who completed a desired action on a single web page, such as filling out a
form. Pages with high conversion rates are performing well, while pages with low conversion rates are
performing poorly.
Cross-Selling
When a sales rep has more than one type of product to offer consumers that could be beneficial, and
s/he successfully sells a consumer more than one item either at the time of purchase or later on. An
example is when Apple sells you an iPhone and then successfully sells you an Apple iPhone case or a
pair of Apple headphones. In this case, a sales rep identifies a need the customer has, and fulfils that
need by recommending an additional product. (Cross-selling differs from up-selling)
Customer Relationship Management (CRM)
Software that let companies keep track of everything they do with their existing and potential customers.
At the simplest level, CRM software lets you keep track of all the contact information for these customers.
But CRM systems can do lots of other things, too, like track email, phone calls, faxes, and deals; send
personalised emails; schedule appointments; and log every instance of customer service and support.
Some systems also incorporate feeds from social media such as Facebook, Twitter, LinkedIn, and others.
The goal is to create a system in which sales reps have a lot of information at their fingertips and can
quickly pull up everything about a prospect or existing customer.
Direct Selling
Direct selling refers to sales that occur through independent sales representatives, usually not from a
store but from the customer’s own location.
Forecasting
Estimating future sales performance for a forecast period based on historical data. Forecasted
performance can vary widely from actual sales results, but helps sales reps plan their upcoming days,
weeks, and months, and helps high-level employees set standards for expenses, profit, and growth.
Lead
A person or company who has shown interest in a product or service in some way, shape, or form.
Perhaps they filled out a form, subscribed to a blog, or shared their contact information.

When going to a sales meeting, or when you are expecting a visit from an interested customer at your

• If you are selling products, you need a full set of samples, clearly marked with branding and codes
where relevant
• A pricing structure that is relevant to the customer you are meeting – wholesale, retail,
corporate, etc. If you sell services, a broad indication of your pricing structure is necessary. This
should include sales terms & conditions.
• Any marketing materials – business cards, catalogues, brochures, prospectus, copies of media
articles, etc that you want to share with the customer.
• A pen and paper. Sounds obvious? I have lost count of the times a potential supplier has asked me to
borrow a pen to write down the details of my order. Not a great way to instil confidence!
• If you are hosting the customer, some tea or coffee, mineral water and perhaps some fruit or biscuits is
always a good idea.
• Cell phones should be on silent, and you should only take calls or allow staff to disturb you if it
is extremely urgent and after you have asked your guest’s permission.
In addition to the physical and environmental preparations, you should also be prepared in terms of
information and knowledge, as highlighted below:
Know thy products & services
In order to avoid getting caught out at a sales meeting, it is critical that you have excellent product
knowledge and understand the ins and outs of your service offering or your finished products. This could
include what is and is not included in the service offer, the raw materials you use, how the end user
can display or use your products and any other relevant information that relates to your products,
service or offering.
Know thy competitors
Any buyer worth their salt will try and throw a few curve balls to unsettle you or create uncertainty in
your mind, in the hope of getting a better deal. Chief amongst these is comparing your offering to other
similar products or services available in the market, by saying things like “JBM Consulting does a full
green audit for 20% cheaper than you have quoted” or “I can source the same products direct from
the East myself at 40% less.”
In this instance, it never pays to be defensive, nor will it serve your purpose to speak badly of your
competition. Rather, point out the benefits of doing business with you as opposed to your competitors –
perhaps your service includes a six-month follow-up, or you are SABS accredited, or maybe you offer a
wider range, or can customise products to suit client needs. Maybe you use better quality materials, or
perhaps it is your service and reputation that sets you apart.

Appendix 3: MANAGING RELATIONSHIPS WITH LARGE RETAILERS
Many of us have a dream to become a supplier to a large retailer like Mr Price, Woolworths or Pick n
Pay. My response to this is: be careful what you wish for, it may just come true!
Large retail chains have a lot to offer – they order in serious volumes, offer incredible exposure and
national distribution. They can also be loyal and repeat customers, especially if the products you make
for them sell well.
BUT (Yes, there is a but...) you need to be aware of the following before entering into a deal with a
large and established retail chain:
• They have very demanding systems for ordering, invoicing, delivery and logistics, and you will
be expected to comply like any other supplier.
• Payment lead times can vary from 14 days (with a 5% settlement discount) to up to 60 or even 90
days. Can your cash flow sustain you while you wait to be paid an order you delivered over 3
months ago?
• They expect almost immediate answers to email and other queries and communications.
• They are very taxing on your time. If you are struggling to cope with your existing workload, don’t
even consider this option unless you can bring in extra resources.
• You will generally need to do all the packing, packaging and labelling on the products. This will entail
you managing the printing of the bar codes, labels, etc.
• They will expect several sets of samples prior to delivery. These are known as red seal or black seal
samples and will be used to assess overall quality of the order on delivery.
As you can see from above, managing relationships with large retailers can be a challenge for a small
business. However, if you have the systems and the inclination, it can be a very rewarding strategy.
Tips for managing large retail relationships:
• Befriend the buyer and nurture this relationship as much as possible.
• Befriend someone in the accounts department. This can often be the difference between
prompt payment and the poor house!
• Communicate regularly. Provide updates on orders, notification of specials or new products,
any relevant marketing info or newsletters, and just general contact. Stay top-of-mind!
• Communicate potential challenges WELL in advance. Many large retailers have penalties for
late delivery, even cancellation in some instances.
• Stick to the agreed colour, size, shape and quality of the samples – in many cases even
slight deviations will not be tolerated and the order can be cancelled or rejected.
• Stick to packaging, invoicing, deliveries, etc. These organisations do not have the time or
inclination to have to make allowances for individual suppliers.
• Manage your cash flow needs with a good finance system.

The service-based business and its sales process
Selling a service generally takes more time and requires you to engage more with potential clients from

customer experience and achieving client satisfaction; as opposed to the core focus of marketing for a

Appendix 8: MARKETING PLAN BASICS
Everyone knows you need a business plan, yet many entrepreneurs don’t realise a marketing plan is
just as vital. A marketing plan focuses on gaining and keeping customers. It’s strategic and includes
numbers, facts and objectives. It spells out the tools and tactics you’ll use to achieve your sales goals.
It’s your plan of action—what you’ll sell, who’ll want to buy it and how you will generate leads that result
in sales. Unless you’re using your marketing plan to help you gain funding, it doesn’t have to be lengthy
or beautifully written. Use bulleted sections and get right to the point. Here’s a closer look at creating
a marketing plan:
Step 1: Provide a snapshot of your company’s current situation (“situation analysis”).
• Define your company and its products or services.
• Show how the benefits you provide set you apart from your competition.
• Provide an overview of your company’s strengths, weaknesses, opportunities and threats.
Strengths and weaknesses exist within your business, while opportunities and threats are outside
factors. For your company’s strengths, consider how its products are superior, or if your service
is more comprehensive. What gives you a competitive advantage? Weaknesses can be e.g.,
from operating in a saturated market to lack of experienced staff.
• Describe external opportunities you can capitalise on, such as an expanding market for your
product. Also include external threats to your company’s ability to gain market share so that
succeeding sections of your plan can detail the ways you’ll overcome these.
Positioning your product involves two steps: you need to analyse your product’s features and decide how
they distinguish your product from its competitors; then decide what type of buyer is most likely to
purchase your product. What are you selling? Convenience? Quality? Discount pricing? You can’t offer it
all. Knowing what your customers want helps you decide what to offer.
Step 2: Describe your target audience.
• Develop a one-paragraph profile of your prospective customer. Describe their demographics:
age, sex, family composition, earnings, geographic location, and lifestyle. Ask: Are my customers
conservative or innovative? Leaders or followers? Timid or aggressive? Traditional or modern?
Introverted or extroverted? How often do they purchase what I offer? In what quantity?
• Business-to-business marketers should define the target audience based on their type of
business, job title, size of business, geographic location or other characteristics that make them
prospects. Define them narrowly, it will be your guide as you plan your media and public
relations campaigns.
Step 3: List your marketing goals.
• What do you want your marketing plan to achieve? List measurable goals so that you’ll know when
you’ve achieved them.
Step 4: Develop the marketing communications strategies and tactics you’ll use.
A good marketing plan targets prospects at all stages of your sales cycle. Many forms of
advertising, public relations and direct marketing, are great for reaching cold prospects. Warm prospects
– those who’ve previously been exposed to your marketing message, and perhaps met you personally –
will respond best to permission-based email, loyalty programmes and customer appreciation events,
for example.
© 2024 Fetola (Pty) Ltd • Phone: 0861111690 • 84
sabfoundationtholoana@fetola.co.za

Outline your primary marketing strategies.
• List the tactics you’ll use to reach prospects at any point in your sales cycle. For example, you might
combine outdoor billboards, print advertising, social media campaigns, and online local searches
to reach cold prospects, but use email to contact your warm prospects.
• Find out which media your target audience turns to for information on the type of product or
service you sell. Avoid broad-based media – even if it attracts your target audience – if the
content isn’t relevant. The marketing tactics you choose must reach your prospects when they’ll
be most receptive to your message.
Step 5: Set your marketing budget.
• Devote a percentage of projected gross sales to your annual marketing budget.
• This may mean using funding, borrowing or self-financing, however, marketing is essential to your
success.
• There are many different tactics for reaching out to every audience niche, so there’s a mix to fit
even the tightest budget.
• After gathering costs for your tactics, you may find you’ve exceeded your budget. Adjust the
tactics until you have a mix that’s affordable. The key is to never stop marketing.
