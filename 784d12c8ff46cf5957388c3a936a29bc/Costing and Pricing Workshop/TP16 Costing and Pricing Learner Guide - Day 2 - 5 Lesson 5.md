DISCOUNTS

An  Achilles  heel  of  the  SME  and  SMME  sector  –  too  often  we  create  a  climate  where  customers  EXPECT  a  discount,
because we are so quick to offer one.

The crux – discounting must be strategic and must also be a win-win arrangement – I give, you give.

The Effects of Discounting

The effects of discounting can be quite dramatic, and very unexpected. For instance if you were selling a product with a
gross profit of 30% and, in order to increase customer sales or beat the competition, you decided to offer a discount of
10%, how many extra customers (as a percentage) do you think you would need to make the same profit overall?

The answer is at least 50% more customers!!

Did you guess correctly - a bit of bad luck if you didn’t? Unfortunately, you can’t afford bad luck in business. In fact, you
may be better off putting the price up slightly, losing some of your core customers and making the same profit (after all,
that’s what you’re in business to do, isn’t it?)

Many new businesses assume that if they sell at less than the competition, they will take their customers away. This is
rarely true and increasing your price, or charging the same but offering a bit more, is often a more sustainable way of
competing profitably.

Consider the following when considering a discounting strategy:

•  Discounting should always be seen as a two-way street & negotiating tool

•  Can the competition discount for longer than you?

•  Will your core customers increase dramatically if you discount? You may be well advised to charge the same and

rather offer a higher level of service

•

If you increased your price by just 5% you could afford to lose 15% of your customers and still make the same profit!

When is it Appropriate to offer a Discount?

Of course, there are certain scenarios and strategies where offering a discount is a reasonable and acceptable business
decision. These include:

•  Quantity  Discounting,  where  the  order  will  be  significantly  increased  as  a  result  of  the  discount  being  offered.
However, do not offer a quantity discount off the bat, use it as a negotiating tool. Similarly, if it costs you exactly the
same to produce one or one hundred of an item, you need to think before offering a quantity discount as it is your
direct profit being discounted.

•  Where a retailer is working according to set price points. Woolworths, Mr Price etc. will TELL YOU what they want
to pay for an item and you need to comply. In this instance, you are well within your rights to look at simplifying or
redesigning the product or service so that it is a win-win situation for all parties – we call this product engineering.

What if your price is not competitive?

If you have done your market and competitor research and find that your prices
are unrealistic, the following should be considered:

•  Can you work more effectively and efficiently, by developing/improving your admin or production systems, or by

simplifying your processes?

•  Can you decrease your overheads or running costs?

•  Are there other potential income streams that could contribute towards your overheads or running costs?

•  Can you focus on a niche market that would be willing to pay more?

•  Can  you  add  value  without  adding  cost,  as  in  offering  a  faster  turnaround  time?  For  such  an  offering  to  have  a

positive impact, it is important that the customer knows about it and values it.

•  Can you re-engineer your product or service and make it more competitive?

Can you think of any other strategies that you can try to make your prices work when they are too high based on your
costing & required profit levels?

or service:

Apart from the materials used or the labour hours that go into the delivery of a product or service, there are several factors
that can also impact your pricing strategy:

Company Profile

The higher your company profile, experience and brand recognition, the more you can charge.

Objectives

Are your objectives to penetrate a market, beat the competition, establish yourself as a luxury brand? These will affect
your pricing significantly.

Client dynamics

If you really want to work with a certain client (such as a big corporate or well-known brand) you might go lower in price;
conversely, if you are expecting a difficult project or a slower payer, then you might want to increase your price.

Expect negotiation

People often add 10% to their initial quoted price so that they can give a discount or negotiate down, as some clients
might expect this. The jury is out on this, it depends on the client.

Product/service Engineering

Sometimes, your client gives a price that they are willing to pay and you let them know what you can do for that fee. This
may entail simplifying your product or service to come into price.

Job satisfaction

Sometimes  we  take  on  work  that  is  not  the  most  profitable,  but  provides  excellent  experience  and  exposure  to  new
thinking.  Other  times,  we  take  on  profitable  work  that  is  mind-numbingly  boring,  soul-destroying  or  even  dangerous.
Always weigh up the pros and cons.

Pricing Services

In some organizations (such as a repair shop or a hairdresser), a consistent hourly rate will be charged regardless of
which  employee  actually  works  on  the  job;  in  other  organizations,  the  rate  may  vary  by  employee.  For  example,  in
an accounting firm, the rate charged for a junior accountant’s time will generally be less than the rate charged for an
experienced senior accountant, or for a partner.

There are a number of different ways of setting prices in a service environment, and it may be useful to quickly look at
some of them here:

Per hour – you determine an hourly rate and charge according to number of hours a given service takes to deliver. You
can set different hourly rates for different tasks, different rates based on who does the work, as well as the complexity of
the work.

Per  job  –  you  set  a  fixed  price  for  a  certain  job,  e.g.  R500  per  game  drive,  R150  per  airport  transfer,  R2500  per
workshop.

Tips on Pricing Services

1.  Test Your Prices with Existing Audiences

“Anytime we roll out a new product or service, we always test the price with a few — we usually limit the number of lists
to three — different existing segmented email lists that signed for that specific topic or a related offer to determine the
best pricing model.” ~ Kristin Kimberly Marquet, Creative Development Agency, LLC

2.  Let the Market Price Your Product

“Pricing can be such a sore spot for many entrepreneurs, but it shouldn’t be. Few realize that the value is in the eye of the
buyer, which means you’ll always make the most money leaving the price up to the customer. Go have conversations —
test prices, ask whether buyers would buy within a range of prices, and explore why they would or wouldn’t. These easy
conversations will make you money.” ~ Peter Kozodoy, GEM Advertising

3.  Start High

“It’s easier to come down in price than to go up. So your first step should be to generate a solid ballpark price. You can
look at similar products or services to do that, then do some testing or polling. Then I would recommend to start on the
high end of that ballpark or even a bit above it. If you need to have a sale later on, customers will appreciate that.” ~
Nicolas Gremion, Free-eBooks.net

4.  Do Competitive Research

“For  us,  it  all  boils  down  to  research.  Learn  what  the  market  is  paying  for  your  new  service  and  learn  what  your
competitors are charging for that service. Once you have that data, you can uniquely position yourself based on your
price point, quality and offering.” ~ Joel Mathew, Fortress Consulting

5.  Figure Out Your Market Positioning

“The most important thing when you price for a service, tool or product is how you want to position yourself in the market.
Once you determine that, consider the price your competitors are charging for it and the number of expected achievable
customers.  Then,  ask  if  are  you  adding  a  value  compared  to  competitors  and  if  you  can  diversify  pricing  by  creating
packages.” ~ Michael Hsu, DeepSky

6.  Start At Twice Your Costs

“Start with a basic model work backward. First, figure out your costs, then aim at a price point of 2C, or twice your costs.
Later, review the marketspace to see what competitive offers and then adjust accordingly. Hopefully, your operation is
tight enough that you will be able to compete on value.” ~ Nicole Munoz, Nicole Munoz Consulting, Inc.

7.  Conduct A/B Tests

“For us, we’ve found that continuous A/B testing is the best way to continue increasing conversions on our pricing page.
Experiment with a 14-day free trial, different packages, and monthly pricing versus yearly pricing. For example, if churn
is an issue for your business, a yearly price could help reduce your attrition rate, but you’ll only find out through testing.”
~ Syed Balkhi, WPBeginner

For each new service-based project a financial proposal or quote should be produced and include:

•  A brief and terms and conditions with your proposal

•

The financial terms & conditions – payment schedules, sundries & expenses etc

•  A request to the client to sign off each stage and for you to invoice them separately for each stage

•  An explanation of each stage of the project

•  What the client will receive at the end of each stage and the final outcomes

•  How long each stage will take

•  How much each stage will cost

•  How any unforeseen circumstances – such as extras – will be charged for

•

Include how long this quote is valid for

•  Ultimately, good communication between you and the client will avoid problems and late or non-payments.
