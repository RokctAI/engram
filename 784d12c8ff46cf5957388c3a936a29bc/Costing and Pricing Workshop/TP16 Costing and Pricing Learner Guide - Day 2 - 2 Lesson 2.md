Pricing does not exist in a vacuum. Pricing strategies reflect not only what it took for you to bring a product to market (hard
costs), but also what your target customers think it is worth (brand value), and what your competitors are charging for
similar products or services. For this reason, unlike costing, pricing is not an exact science. You need to do your research
and then use all the information at your disposal to make an informed decision when calculating your prices.

Remember, the ‘Right’ Price is the one that people are prepared to pay, and that also delivers you the necessary profit
you require.

Below we will look at some accepted strategies, tools and methods to help guide you to the Right Price for your products
and services. Let’s start on our journey…

COST PLUS

Cost plus pricing is the simplest method one can use for determining prices, and is the most commonly applied.  In this
method, you simply take the cost of the product and add a mark-up to create a selling price. You can do this by using
a  fixed  percentage  (+100%,  +200%)  or  a  fixed  markup  (+R50,  +R100),  with  a  percentage  mark-up  being  the  most
common.

The advantage of this method is that it is simple, and you can calculate your expected profit level very easily. You can
work out exactly what you stand to make per product, per workshop, per contract, for an entire harvest etc, and can thus
make accurate profit projections - as long as your sales targets are met.

The  disadvantage  is  that  it  often  may  be  less,  or  more,  than  customers  are  willing  to  pay,  and  if  one  is  too  rigid  in
employing this method some opportunities will be lost, either because you price yourself out of the market, or because
you miss an opportunity to maximize your profit.

Below is a simple example of cost-plus pricing:

Cost of materials

+ Cost of labour

+ Overhead @ 50%

= Total cost

+ Desired profit (50% on costs)

= Final selling price

The next milestone along your journey of arriving at the Right Price, involves understanding how your chosen sector and
the customers that make it up relate to price. What does your experience or research tell you about your target market
and their buying impulses?

If a ‘cheap’ price is the most important thing to them, then you may even have to sell for less than your cost-plus pricing
is telling you or look at ways of simplifying the product/service to bring costs down so that you can offer a lower price.

at low or discounted rates. In fact, you may even put customers off – when products are perceived as being ‘too cheap’,
especially at the upper end of the market, this can be a turn off; people may think there is something wrong with the item,
it is poorly made, has little value, nobody wants to buy it etc.

This perception also holds true for organizations that offer services – if you are asking a potential customer to support
your consulting business and are offering sessions at R75 each, he or she may well question the quality of the offering
being provided…similarly, imagine a legal firm that charges out its services at R50/hour – you would probably rather
pay ten times that to avoid going to jail!

Remember that price itself is often a measure of value – we automatically value a Mercedes Benz more than a Tazz
because it is expensive, not only because it has more features.

When using your analysis of the Market as a guide to set prices, you need to consider the following:

•

Is your product/service just one of many or is it unique, or very rare? Many luxury brands for example, maintain high
pricing levels by limiting the availability of a particular product.

•  What type/demographic of customer are you targeting? (tourists, students, local residents, government, corporates,

resellers etc.)? How do they generally respond to price?

•  What  segment  of  the  market  are  you  wanting  to  be  positioned  in  (Premium,  Luxury,  Middle-Income,  Cut-price)?  Is
your offering properly positioned to penetrate that market? This speaks to branding, packaging, service levels etc.

By looking at this information and answering the above questions, you should be able to determine a guide price bracket
for your service or product that the market is willing to pay.

When  you  combine  this  with  the  other  information  at  your  disposal  (cost  plus  figures,  competitor  pricing,  supply  &
demand), you will be able to arrive at the most appropriate price – BUT remember, unlike costing, pricing is STILL not an
exact science…At the end of the day, the right price is the one the customer is prepared to pay, that still allows you to
make sufficient profit.

COMPETITOR PRICING

Once you have an idea of your costs and a target mark-up figure from a cost-plus perspective and have done your market
research in terms of how your customers relate to price, the next step is to determine competitor pricing and how this
influences what you can charge. As the name implies, this involves researching how much the competition is charging for
similar products or services in your industry. In order to do this, you may have to telephone them for quotes, do research
online or ask a colleague or member of your family to pose as a buyer to obtain a quote….

The information you gather on what others are charging will give you a benchmark to compare against. When combined
with the results of your market research, you should be empowered to make an informed decision as to what you are
able to charge.

It is generally unwise to price your product much higher or lower than the competition without a good reason – too high
and you may lose customers (as you price yourself out of the market), too low and you are giving away profit and also
suggesting  that  your  offering  is  inferior...There  are  some  exceptions,  such  as  penetration  pricing,  but  the  general  rule
stands.

a superior customer experience, a more comprehensive warranty policy, an obviously higher quality product, or a more
powerful and compelling brand promise.

Remember when comparing your offering against the competition, consider the following:

•

Is their product/service the same as, or more convenient to access than yours? Is it in more outlets and easier to find?
Do they have national reach perhaps?

•  Do they offer anything extra that you do not at this stage? (eg after-sales service, warranty, guarantees, free delivery,

accreditation standards etc)

•

Is their branding/packaging/merchandising/marketing/brand awareness better than yours? What about their online
presence and social media?

In other words, you need to really (really!) compare apples with apples when doing your Competitor Pricing analysis
and comparing your price with theirs!

SUPPLY AND DEMAND

Just before you set your final prices, it is always important to remember that one of the major drivers of price is supply and
demand. Simply put, the less supply (or product available for sale) and more demand (or buyers wishing to purchase) for
a particular product, the higher the price people are prepared to pay for it.

Look  at  the  price  of  citrus  fruit  –  in  season  there  are  plenty  of  oranges  and  the  price  is  low.  Out  of  season  there  is  a
shortage and the price increases, sometimes as much as threefold or even more.  It is the same with most products. If too
many people in an area are selling bottled water, or offering computer training, or making school uniforms, customers
will demand lower prices and sellers will ultimately have to ‘discount’ to make a sale.

If this is a challenge you face, your job is to develop products & services that are original, unique and different to what
everyone else is selling – innovate, don’t replicate! A second strategy is to add value without necessarily adding to the
selling price – what can you offer that the competition is not? Free delivery perhaps? Or a warranty? Perhaps some kind
of a loyalty programme? Thirdly, you always need to concentrate on building a powerful brand, because the value that
people attach to your brand will translate directly into a better selling price and a more loyal customer base.

A focus on innovation and value-add will help ensure that you are able to command a fair price for your products, while
a focus on brand building will help you get a great price. This applies whether you are in the farming sector, consulting,
services – what can you do differently, and how can you add value to your customer?

Do you think setting prices like R99 or R499 is cheesy and pointless?  Think again.  If you have a product or service that
should be sold at R100 (or even R110), you have two options.  Either drop it below R100, and you’ll see sales pick up.
Or, if you don’t want it that cheap, raise it up to R125.  In general, R125 and R110 feel about the same to people, so
you’ll sell the same amount and make R15 extra profit each time.

Remember, you’re not exempt if you’re selling a service.  If you’re a consultant, people will perceive your services as
significantly more affordable at R385/hour, than at R400.  If you’re a web developer, people are more likely to hire you
if you quote R14,700 for a job instead of R15,000.  We are not 100% certain why this is the case, it is just the way the
human brain works….

Interestingly, the one area where this strategy does not seem to hold sway is at the premium/luxury goods level. Research
shows that at this level customers respond more to rounded-off prices such as R1000 or R5000…

BE WISE ABOUT RAISING YOUR PRICES

It’s  a  fact  of  life  that  you  will  have  to  raise  prices  from  time  to  time.  If  you  never  raise  your  prices,  you  won’t  stay  in
business for long. You have to constantly monitor your prices and your costs so that you are competitive in the market,
and that you make the kind of money you need to make.

That said, there are risks to raising prices, particularly when your customers themselves are going through tough financial
times. Recent food and petrol hikes have everyone twitchy about everything going up, so take into account the following
when you have to raise your prices:

Some Tips around Raising Prices:

•  Do raise prices when your competitors are raising prices. If the competition has upped the ante, that is a good

signal that the market can and will support a price increase for your services, too.

•  Do raise prices if your customers say you’re a bargain. If your customers keep commenting about what a great

value your prices are, that may be an indication you’re charging too little…

•  Do raise prices if you introduce a new product or service. But only if you need to, not just for the sake of it.

•  Don’t raise prices too much all at once. In a tough economy, a big jump in prices might be too much of a jolt for
your customers. Instead, raise prices in small increments of two or three price increases over the course of a year.

•  Don’t  raise  prices  across  the  board.  Customers  may  not  notice  price  increases  if  they  are  only  for  certain

products or services and not for others.

A new strategy that a lot of retailers are pursuing is Bundling, whereby they offer a better price if you buy three of an item
as an example. This is a strategy that can sometimes offset rising costs by driving volumes.
