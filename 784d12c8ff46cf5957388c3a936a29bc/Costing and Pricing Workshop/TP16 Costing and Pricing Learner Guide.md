In our experience, costing is one of the most important components of any business.
Consider the following:
1. Without an accurate indication of how much an item costs to produce, or a service costs to provide, it is impossible
to know how much profit you are making (if any), and thus if your business model is sustainable.
2. Knowing your costs makes planning, forecasting and cash flow management far easier.
3. Knowing your costs makes cost control and cost reduction easier, as you can identify problems at their source.
4. Knowing your costings means you can identify your most (and least) productive processes, products and services and
make adjustments accordingly.
5. Accurate costings empower you when it comes to negotiating with customers – if you know how much a product
or service really costs the business, you will know how much, if at all, you are able to discount in order to secure a deal.

3. LESSON ONE:
3.1 COSTING TERMINOLOGY
• Cost is a measurement, in monetary terms, of the amount of resources used for the purpose of production of
goods or rendering services.
• Cost is the amount of actual or notional expenditure relating to a product, service, process or activity.
• Cost is often used as a generic term to describe various types of costs.
• Costing is the technique and process of ascertaining costs.
Costing is the “science” of ensuring that all inputs to the delivery of a product or service are covered.
Direct costs
All transport, labour and materials directly used in the manufacture of your product or the delivery of your service.
Generally, if you are not making stock, producing an order or delivering a service, you are not incurring direct costs.
These costs tend to vary month to month depending on your volu me of work and are sometimes called variable costs.
Overheads
These are the running costs that one incurs whether you are making products, providing services or not – hence them also
sometimes being called fixed costs. This includes things such as the cost of labour not directly involved in production or
service delivery but needed to run the business (such as administrative staff, sales & marketing staff, cleaners etc), as well
as all recurring and ad hoc running costs including electricity, internet, insurance, rent, phones, legal services, stationery,
depreciation etc.
Cost of Goods Sold
Cost of goods sold (COGS) are the direct costs attributable to the production of the goods sold by a company. This amount
includes the hard costs of the materials and direct labour used to produce the goods or deliver the service, or all the direct
costs associated with purchasing stock for resale as in a retail environment. It excludes indirect expenses (overheads).
COGS appears on the income statement and can be deducted from revenue to calculate a company’s gross margin (GP).
In manufacturing we call these Cost of Goods Sold, in retail it is usually referred to as “Cost of Sales.” This is essentially
the same thing as Direct Costs, except sometimes COGS will include an apportionment for factory overheads, or storage
of stock items.
Margins
Profit margin indicates the profitability of a product, service, or business. It’s expressed as a percentage; the higher
the number, the more profitable the business.
Gross Profit Margin, or gross margin, is the difference between total sales and the direct cost/cost of sales of those goods.
For example: If total sales equals R1,000 and cost of sales equals R300, then the gross margin equals R700 (or 70%).
Net Profit margin refers to the percentage of revenue remaining after all costs, depreciation, interest, taxes, and
other expenses have been deducted. The formula is: (Total Sales - Total Expenses)/Total Sales x 100 = Net Profit
Margin as a percentage.

Break-Even Point
A level where the amount of sales attained is met with the same amount of costs, resulting in zero profit or loss. A formula
(which is in your addenda) can be used to determine the break-even point for a business.
Depreciation
Depreciation is a concept used for tax and accounting purposes that describes the method a company uses to account for
the declining value of its assets. The reasoning? An asset acquired this year is unlikely to be worth as much in five years
because it often will have worn down, been depleted, or become obsolete.
Depreciation is an accounting convention that allows a company to write off an asset’s value over time, but it is considered
a non-cash transaction.
As a benchmark, most general assets are depreciated at a rate of around 20 – 25% per year.

3.2 TYPES OF COSTS
DIRECT (OR VARIABLE) COSTS
• These are costs that are only incurred if you work on a particular product, job or order, and include all material costs
(including things such as venue hire, accommodation or catering if you provide a service), direct labour and any
other materials (eg boxes, packaging, pallets, printouts) required to complete that particular product, job or order.
• You can also include transport as a direct cost, provided it relates specifically to that job or contract eg physical
delivery. General transport would be listed as an overhead (see below).
• In some cases, you may find it appropriate and useful to allocate items such as electricity usage as a direct cost, if
these are able to be measured accurately and traced to a specific product/project – such as when using a kiln or a
commercial oven that can be monitored.
OVERHEADS (FIXED COSTS)
• Overheads/fixed costs are costs which are incurred whether you are producing orders, selling products, providing
services or not. They may fluctuate slightly month to month depending on work volumes, but in general these are
stable, recurring costs.
• Overheads are frequently left out of the costing process or miscalculated, leading to an incorrect final costing and
potential losses as a result. They need to be accounted for.
• Overheads include costs like:
o Telephone and internet accounts
o Advertising and promotional costs
o Electricity and water
o Rent/bond/rates
o Accounting and other business services
o Fixed salaries and wages (marketing, finances, admin & accounts etc)
o Vehicle and machinery maintenance and depreciation – usually calculated as a percentage of the total value of
the item/s
o Insurance – theft, fire, key man, loss of earnings
o Consumables (computer software, small hand tools, machinery repairs and renewals)
o General transport and fuel costs
o Staff training and development
o Uniforms & PPE wear
o Etc
NOTE: Some businesses divide their overheads up into Manufacturing/Factory Overheads and Administrative
Overheads. For this workshop, we will combine these two into one figure.

3.3 OVERHEAD ALLOWANCE OR OVERHEAD CONTRIBUTION
Because in most instances it is nearly impossible to apportion exact overhead usage to a specific product or service,
a viable option is to use an Overhead Contribution to apportion overhead costs. It is called an overhead contribution
because every product sold or service delivered by your organisation needs to give something back/contribute towards
covering the running costs of the business, by being included in the costing calculation process.
So how do we record it?
The most simple and accurate way of recording an overhead contribution, is to express it as a percentage. This percentage
is added to your direct costs (direct labour & materials) to give you a TOTAL COST to deliver that product or service - per
product made, per learner trained, per shipment delivered, per pump serviced, per contract executed etc. The formula
looks like this below:
Direct Costs (Labour & Materials) + Fixed Costs (Overhead Contribution as a %)
= Total Cost of Production/Delivery of Service
How much should you add?
Every business has different overhead and cost structures, so there is no ‘one-size-fits-all’ answer. In a service-based
business, your main costs are usually staff related, so the figure will depend on how large and expensive your team is.
In a production environment, a typical overhead figure would range anywhere from 25% to 70%, and in agriculture this
could be even higher due to the need to recoup running costs for expensive machinery and equipment, depreciation,
maintenance and other general running costs.
The figure is generally slightly less for the services sector, which although quite heavy in terms of staff costs as indicated
above, usually has less equipment & machinery costs, less need for a large production or workspace, lower electricity
usage etc than a large-scale manufacturer with a big factory or a farmer, for example.
At the end of the day, your overhead contribution % all depends on how high your overheads are that need to be
recovered every month/quarter/year.
For the purpose of this workshop, we will use an overhead contribution of 25% - this is a bit lower than average but will
serve our purpose as an example in our calculations.
As the formula above shows, this % figure is added to your direct costs to get to the total cost of the product or service
being costed. In this way, every product you sell, or every service you provide, gives back (contributes) a portion towards
the running costs of your organisation.

cash flow issues
•   If you know your overheads and direct costs, you can accurately work out exact profits per product, per

4. LESSON TWO:
4.1 COSTING METHODOLOGIES AND PROCESSES:
Costing Methodologies
• Different types of companies & sectors use different costing methods. A job costing method is used when costs can be
traced to a specific job such as building a house, catering for an event, doing an environmental impact assessment
(EIA) or designing and printing an annual report. This is the most common method, especially for SMEs.
• But imagine a company mass-producing corn flakes, or facial tissues. Here it would be very difficult to trace exact
costs to an individual box of corn flakes or a box of facial tissues. This company would most probably rather use
process costing, an accounting system that calculates costs involved along a specific production process. This is
usually the accepted method for costing mass produced products or large production chains.
• In this workshop, and because it is the more common solution in the SME and SMME space, we will focus primarily
on job costing as a methodology. If you want to learn more about process costing, you can visit http://www.
accountingtools.com/overview-process-costing
Costing Processes
• Raw materials are costed by weight, by number (quantity) or a combination of both.
• Sometimes when costing materials, we need to estimate usage for items that are difficult to quantify or measure, such
as glue, paint, sewing cotton, certain food ingredients etc... this is essentially an educated guess, and we call this an
allowance. It will usually be expressed as a Rand figure eg R2.00.
• You need to use excel or a similar programme, or at the very least a physical costing sheet and filing system to record
all completed product & service costings for future reference. This will save you having to recost things all the time or
do repeat costings for very similar products or services.
• Remember to update your costs should your suppliers raise their prices! Also, always try and keep general manufacturing
costs as low as possible by minimising wastage. The concept of circularity, looks at ways of eliminating waste in your
processes.
• Make sure all materials included in your costing are available when you need them. Having to substitute materials can
seriously impact costs, delivery deadlines, profit & competitiveness. If this is a potential risk, build it into your quote.
Remember:
• Materials can be costed by weight, by quantity or a combination of both
• We sometimes need to estimate use and this is called an allowance
• Do not offer products or services that utilise materials and elements that may be hard to get in bulk if
you receive a large order!

4.2 COSTING DIRECT MATERIAL USAGE - CALCULATING RATINGS
A rating is the quantity of a particular raw material that goes into an item. A rating tells us exactly how much wood, or
sand, or fabric, or soap or even flour or curry powder we need to make a certain product, and then helps us easily work
out what that raw material actually costs us, per product.
Ratings are best calculated using a Four Step process:
STEP ONE Step One is to decide how you measure that particular material that you are costing. Is it easiest to
measure it by kilograms, or by the metre, or by litre, or by piece? Always choose the one that reflects
how you buy the material in question.
STEP TWO Step Two is to assign a cost to that unit of measurement eg R50/kg or R100/m. We suggest you round
off to make things easier – even if you buy 50 kg or 100 metres at a time – rather work out the cost for
1 kg or 1 metre, it is almost always simpler and more efficient from a costing perspective.
STEP THREE Step Three is to work out how much of each material you need to make a product. The most important
thing, is to know how many of that product can be made from that metre of material, or out of that
kilogram of flour, or from a square metre of aluminium etc.
This may entail laying out the pattern on a metre of fabric and estimating, or actually making up some
samples and recording exact usage. Either way, it is key that you know how many items you can make from
that 1kg of flour, or 1 square metre of wood (based on the unit of measurement you chose in Step One).
Once you have worked this out, you can calculate your rating as follows:
If the item uses LESS THAN one unit of measurement i.e. less than a metre, kg etc. we apply the following
formula:
1 ÷ how many we can produce from that unit of measurement = our rating
Conversely, if the item you are making uses MORE THAN one unit of the material we are measuring, your
rating is simply how much it uses. It is crucial that you understand when to use the above formula, and
when to simply capture how many units of measurement the item uses.
So, if you need 6 buttons to make a shirt, your rating is simply 6. If you need one zip, your rating is 1.
If you need 25m of leather to make a lounge suite, your rating for leather is 25 - and so on.
NB: you only ever need to use the above formula if you can make more than one product from a unit of
measurement (one metre of fabric, one kg of sand etc). If you cannot, your rating is simply how much
(or how many) units of measurement you need to make the product e.g. 20kg of clay – rating is 20; 3m
of fabric, rating is 3.
STEP FOUR THE LAST STEP is to work out actual cost of that particular material when making the product being costed.
To do this, we simply multiply our rating by the cost per metre/kg/unit of the material to get our answer:

5. LESSON THREE
5.1 DIRECT LABOUR COSTS (DIRECT COST)
When calculating the cost of your Direct Labour for a certain product, contract or job, in other words labour that you must
pay for in terms of time or money to complete a certain order or deliver a particular service, there are several things to
remember:
• You need to know beforehand how long it will take to complete a certain product or deliver a certain service (or even
a tender or a contract) before you can arrive at an accurate labour costing. This applies whether you pay by the
hour, by the job or per contract, and whether you make/deliver the product or service yourself or pay multiple other
staff or service providers to do so.
• If you pay your service providers, employees or subcontractors per job, you will still need to have determined an
hourly rate that both you and the subcontractor are happy with before production/implementation begins. The actual
payment per product or service delivered will usually then be drawn from how long that product or service reasonably
takes to do x the agreed hourly rate.
• You can pay/allocate different labour rates for different skills, according to their level of difficulty and/or availability
or scarcity.
Examples:
If a training workshop takes 4 hours and the agreed hourly rate with the trainer is R250/hr, he will be due R250 x 4 =
R1000 upon completion.
If paying a contracted seamstress per item and a garment takes 2 hours to sew from start to finish, and the agreed rate for
that type of product or skill is R100/hour, then R200 would be a fair price to pay per completed item. If a highly-skilled
producer can make the item in 1.5 hours, they should still be paid R200 – you need to set the rate at an average skill
level and not at the highest or lowest…
• In a manufacturing/production set-up it is usually more efficient and cost effective to use different producers for stages
of the production process, instead of having one producer make the whole item. The time of each stage will need to
be known or at the very least accurately estimated if you are to ensure accurate costings, and also if you need to pay
different producers for their particular contribution along the production line.
• In many cases, it is wise to work out a minute rate for each producer/employee, especially if a product involves
several small steps in the production process. This is how much it costs the company, per minute, for that employee’s
time. Services, by contrast will usually be costed on an hourly, daily or even weekly basis.
To work out a minute rate, start with the agreed daily wage and then work backwards as follows:
• Agreed Daily Rate = R500
R500 ÷ 7hrs = R71.43 per hour (avg 8-hour workday with an hour for breaks deducted)
R71.43 ÷ 60 minutes = R1.19c per minute (This is the employee’s minute rate)

CALCULATING DIRECT LABOUR RATES FOR SALARIED EMPLOYEES
Direct manufacturing labour costs are the total costs of staff who work directly on goods being manufactured. It is a
useful measurement for a small business to use because it allows you to understand exactly how much of your wage bill
is going toward employees who actually manufacture your products. Calculate this cost by identifying direct labourers
and computing their wages:
1. Determine which workers are considered direct labourers. Direct labourers are workers who directly perform work
on the product being produced. For example, in a bakery the person who mixes the dough or bakes the bread is a
direct labourer, but the person who sweeps the floors is not…
2. Add up the total hours that all direct labourers work in the period in question (per month, per quarter or per year).
If you had four direct labourers who worked 400, 300, 200 and 500 hours, you would add these hours up to get
1,400 hours.
3. Multiply the number of hours worked over the period by their labour rate. This will give you the total direct labour
costs. Assuming that your wage rate is R50 per hour, you would multiply it by 1,400 -- the number of hours in this
example -- to get total direct manufacturing costs of R70 000 for the period. You could then calculate this as a
percentage of your total wage bill and get a ratio of direct vs indirect labour in your company.
DIRECT LABOUR COSTING – THINGS TO REMEMBER:
• If you manufacture or deal in any goods that need to be packed, you need to remember to cost in any labour you pay
for in terms of packing and packaging of products – this is a cost centre that many ignore or forget about.
• Make sure you are familiar with your rights & obligations as an employer, both to protect yourself in case of any
problems, and also to ensure your employees are pulling their weight!
• If you make use of subcontractors for all or part of your manufacturing or service provision process, the terms and
amounts of payment must be agreed up front in writing and quality standards, deadlines, policies in terms of reject
products or poor service delivery must be discussed and agreed upon as well. Have a contract or at least an SLA
(Service Level Agreement) in place.
• You need to ensure that your organisation makes a profit from the money it spends on labour.
• If you pay people per day or per week, be sure that they stick to the times that have been allowed for production in the
costing – this usually entails setting tangible production targets and ensuring that these are followed. You may even
want to use incentives to encourage productivity. This also applies to the delivery of a service – if you have allowed
for 5hrs to do a certain piece of work or perform a certain function, you need to ensure it is completed within that
time frame....
• Costs like weekly or monthly salaries and wages paid to staff members who are not directly involved in physical
production or actual delivery of services must be costed as overheads and not as direct labour. This includes marketing
functions, admin, sales, accounts etc.

6.1 COSTING YOUR SERVICE
How do you work out your costs when you have an organization that generates its income by selling time or offering
services such as training, consulting or counselling?
Probably the most critical thing you need to do when running a service organisation, is to charge your time out at the
correct rate. Many organisations find themselves in a situation where they simply are not charging enough per hour or
per job, and therefore making no profit.
Hourly Rate or Flat Fee?
Many service businesses tend to charge by the hour, rather than a flat fee (a fee for the entire job as a whole).
A flat fee can be used, however, when you know with some accuracy how long a job will take to do. An example of this
might be a hairdresser, who knows through experience how long a haircut or certain treatment takes, a service technician
who does a monthly service, or a computer trainer or piano teacher, who have a set rate per lesson.
For many service businesses, though, it can be difficult to work out exactly how long a job will take, hence the usefulness
of an hourly rate. But how do you know what hourly rate to charge?
Method for working out an hourly rate in a service-based business
When working out an hourly rate, the first thing to consider is how many chargeable hours you (and your staff) can achieve.
Let’s imagine you own an environmental consulting business, made up of yourself and two consultants who work for you.
Say, for example, you normally work a 40-hour week. Not all of this time is productive. There will be down time for
things such as lunch breaks, morning and afternoon teas, administration work, doing quotes for clients etc.
Also, don’t forget that you need to allow time for holidays, sick leave, etc.

• Usually if a service business is unprofitable, it is due to a charge-out rate that is too low.

• The charge out rate you work out will need to be updated regularly, as overheads change. If you don’t do this, it will

• You also need to adjust if you take on more staff – as this will alter both the chargeable hours available as well as

to a service-based offering, in which time is the commodity on offer.
6.2 COSTING IN THE AGRICULTURE SECTOR
Costing in Agriculture follows similar rules to any other sector, but it does come with a couple of differences, which makes
it an interesting case study. Below we discuss four main ones:
1. Length of the production cycle can extend across many months, or even years
2. Relationship between inputs and outputs is not fixed, and is influenced by factors out of the farmer’s control (eg
temperature, day length, rainfall)
3. High capital investment costs for land and equipment require repayment over an extended period
4. Fixed pricing and market monopolies limit pricing options for certain produce.

1. Lengthy Production Cycles. Certain crops, for example Macadamia nuts, cattle and oranges only begin
bearing fruit many months and even years into the cycle. The longer the gap between the cost of starting the farm
(planting the trees or buying the cows) the greater the need for capital to cover cashflow deficit. Careful planning
and budgeting is needed to ensure the farm doesn’t run out of money before the harvest happens and income starts
to flow into the business.
2. Variable Input: output relationship. Unlike simple manufacturing costing where one bracelet always uses 5
beads, the outputs in farming (e.g. chicken weight, time to harvest and yield) are determined not only by the inputs
(fertiliser, seeds, water, food) but by other factors out of the farmer’s control. This is one of the reasons why farming
is such a tricky industry to be in, and the reason why farmers invest in irrigation, lighting and greenhouses – to try
and control these other variables. The lack of a fixed input: output relationship also means that whilst costing can be
forecast using a historical budget (ie. what results you got in the past) it also needs to be costed on a real-time basis
- to check your actual results and price each time and with every harvest.
3. High Capital Cost. Farming is notorious for its high capital cost – especially if you need to purchase land at
commercial prices. In addition, the cost of buildings, irrigation equipment and tractors adds a huge price to the
production cost. It is important that these are included in the costing models – but divided over a realistic period.
Land for example is usually repaid over 25 years, tractors and buildings over 10 years and other equipment over a
shorter period. Even if your land and equipment has been paid for already, your costing must build in the price of
replacing this equipment, or you will have no funds to replace the buildings and machinery when they become old.
4. Fixed pricing in agriculture is a challenge that often requires the farmer to reduce their cost of production in
order to make a profit. The dairy industry is notorious for this worldwide, with the market price for milk fixed below
breakeven in many first world countries – requiring farmers to exist on subsidies. Locally this impacts the small farmer
who is dictated to by the market, which is much bigger and stronger than he/she is. Strategies for better pricing
include producing ‘out of season’ when fewer people are selling and prices are higher, doing value-adds such as
packaging and processing, and producing special, quality or unusual products – for example black tomatoes, green
cauliflower and red runner beans.
6.3 COSTING BASICS – SEASONAL FINANCE: INTEREST
Most commercial farmers have to present a production budget to the bank every year with a request for seasonal finance. This
budget includes an estimate of the inputs (seed, fertiliser, electricity etc) and the projected yield and price showing income.
The bank manager needs to be convinced that that the farmer is a ‘good risk’ and there is a good chance that he will
repay the loan once the harvest has been made.
Seasonal finance will be given to the farmer to purchase the inputs, pay labour, pay electricity and all other costs for the
entire growing season, and interest is calculated on the total overdraft on a monthly basis.
These interest costs need to be included in your costing, to ensure that you make a profit at the end. Challenges arise
when a crop sale is delayed or when yields and/or prices are lower than expected, as bank managers generally just
want their money on time!

2.1 PRICING METHODOLOGIES AND STRATEGIES
Pricing does not exist in a vacuum. Pricing strategies reflect not only what it took for you to bring a product to market (hard
costs), but also what your target customers think it is worth (brand value), and what your competitors are charging for
similar products or services. For this reason, unlike costing, pricing is not an exact science. You need to do your research
and then use all the information at your disposal to make an informed decision when calculating your prices.
Remember, the ‘Right’ Price is the one that people are prepared to pay, and that also delivers you the necessary profit
you require.
Below we will look at some accepted strategies, tools and methods to help guide you to the Right Price for your products
and services. Let’s start on our journey…
2.1.1 Cost Plus
Cost plus pricing is the simplest method one can use for determining prices, and is the most commonly applied. In this
method, you simply take the cost of the product and add a mark-up to create a selling price. You can do this by using
a fixed percentage (+100%, +200%) or a fixed markup (+R50, +R100), with a percentage mark-up being the most
common.
The advantage of this method is that it is simple, and you can calculate your expected profit level very easily. You can
work out exactly what you stand to make per product, per workshop, per contract, for an entire harvest etc, and can thus
make accurate profit projections - as long as your sales targets are met.
The disadvantage is that it often may be less, or more, than customers are willing to pay, and if one is too rigid in
employing this method some opportunities will be lost, either because you price yourself out of the market, or because
you miss an opportunity to maximize your profit.
Below is a simple example of cost-plus pricing:
Cost of materials R50.00
+ Cost of labour R30.00
+ Overhead @ 50% R40.00
= Total cost R120.00
+ Desired profit (50% on costs) R60.00
= Final selling price R180.00
2.1.2 MARKET PRICING
The next milestone along your journey of arriving at the Right Price, involves understanding how your chosen sector and
the customers that make it up relate to price. What does your experience or research tell you about your target market
and their buying impulses?
If a ‘cheap’ price is the most important thing to them, then you may even have to sell for less than your cost-plus pricing
is telling you or look at ways of simplifying the product/service to bring costs down so that you can offer a lower price.
© 2024 Fetola (Pty) Ltd • Phone: 0861111690 • sabfoundationtholoana@fetola.co.za

However, if a bargain price is not a major priority to your target market, then you will be throwing away money by selling
at low or discounted rates. In fact, you may even put customers off – when products are perceived as being ‘too cheap’,
especially at the upper end of the market, this can be a turn off; people may think there is something wrong with the item,
it is poorly made, has little value, nobody wants to buy it etc.
This perception also holds true for organizations that offer services – if you are asking a potential customer to support
your consulting business and are offering sessions at R75 each, he or she may well question the quality of the offering
being provided…similarly, imagine a legal firm that charges out its services at R50/hour – you would probably rather
pay ten times that to avoid going to jail!
Remember that price itself is often a measure of value – we automatically value a Mercedes Benz more than a Tazz
because it is expensive, not only because it has more features.
When using your analysis of the Market as a guide to set prices, you need to consider the following:
• Is your product/service just one of many or is it unique, or very rare? Many luxury brands for example, maintain high
pricing levels by limiting the availability of a particular product.
• What type/demographic of customer are you targeting? (tourists, students, local residents, government, corporates,
resellers etc.)? How do they generally respond to price?
• What segment of the market are you wanting to be positioned in (Premium, Luxury, Middle-Income, Cut-price)? Is
your offering properly positioned to penetrate that market? This speaks to branding, packaging, service levels etc.
By looking at this information and answering the above questions, you should be able to determine a guide price bracket
for your service or product that the market is willing to pay.
When you combine this with the other information at your disposal (cost plus figures, competitor pricing, supply &
demand), you will be able to arrive at the most appropriate price – BUT remember, unlike costing, pricing is STILL not an
exact science…At the end of the day, the right price is the one the customer is prepared to pay, that still allows you to
make sufficient profit.
2.1.3 COMPETITOR PRICING
Once you have an idea of your costs and a target mark-up figure from a cost-plus perspective and have done your market
research in terms of how your customers relate to price, the next step is to determine competitor pricing and how this
influences what you can charge. As the name implies, this involves researching how much the competition is charging for
similar products or services in your industry. In order to do this, you may have to telephone them for quotes, do research
online or ask a colleague or member of your family to pose as a buyer to obtain a quote….
The information you gather on what others are charging will give you a benchmark to compare against. When combined
with the results of your market research, you should be empowered to make an informed decision as to what you are
able to charge.
It is generally unwise to price your product much higher or lower than the competition without a good reason – too high
and you may lose customers (as you price yourself out of the market), too low and you are giving away profit and also
suggesting that your offering is inferior...There are some exceptions, such as penetration pricing, but the general rule
stands.

Remember that getting into price-cutting wars with competitors is a sure way to get burnt – if the competitor is bigger and
more established than you, they will be able to bully you out of the market, and if you are equal in size you will both
simply lose profits. Nobody wins.
Should you wish to charge more than your competitors, be ready to make a case for your higher price, such as providing
a superior customer experience, a more comprehensive warranty policy, an obviously higher quality product, or a more
powerful and compelling brand promise.
Remember when comparing your offering against the competition, consider the following:
• Is their product/service the same as, or more convenient to access than yours? Is it in more outlets and easier to find?
Do they have national reach perhaps?
• Do they offer anything extra that you do not at this stage? (eg after-sales service, warranty, guarantees, free delivery,
accreditation standards etc)
• Is their branding/packaging/merchandising/marketing/brand awareness better than yours? What about their online
presence and social media?
In other words, you need to really (really!) compare apples with apples when doing your Competitor Pricing analysis
and comparing your price with theirs!
2.1.4 SUPPLY AND DEMAND
Just before you set your final prices, it is always important to remember that one of the major drivers of price is supply and
demand. Simply put, the less supply (or product available for sale) and more demand (or buyers wishing to purchase) for
a particular product, the higher the price people are prepared to pay for it.
Look at the price of citrus fruit – in season there are plenty of oranges and the price is low. Out of season there is a
shortage and the price increases, sometimes as much as threefold or even more. It is the same with most products. If too
many people in an area are selling bottled water, or offering computer training, or making school uniforms, customers
will demand lower prices and sellers will ultimately have to ‘discount’ to make a sale.
If this is a challenge you face, your job is to develop products & services that are original, unique and different to what
everyone else is selling – innovate, don’t replicate! A second strategy is to add value without necessarily adding to the
selling price – what can you offer that the competition is not? Free delivery perhaps? Or a warranty? Perhaps some kind
of a loyalty programme? Thirdly, you always need to concentrate on building a powerful brand, because the value that
people attach to your brand will translate directly into a better selling price and a more loyal customer base.
A focus on innovation and value-add will help ensure that you are able to command a fair price for your products, while
a focus on brand building will help you get a great price. This applies whether you are in the farming sector, consulting,
services – what can you do differently, and how can you add value to your customer?

distributor), in the form of a quantity discount - the greater the volume of orders they offer (Demand), the lower the price

2.1.5 PSYCHOLOGICAL PRICE POINTS
Do you think setting prices like R99 or R499 is cheesy and pointless? Think again. If you have a product or service that
should be sold at R100 (or even R110), you have two options. Either drop it below R100, and you’ll see sales pick up.
Or, if you don’t want it that cheap, raise it up to R125. In general, R125 and R110 feel about the same to people, so
you’ll sell the same amount and make R15 extra profit each time.
Remember, you’re not exempt if you’re selling a service. If you’re a consultant, people will perceive your services as
significantly more affordable at R385/hour, than at R400. If you’re a web developer, people are more likely to hire you
if you quote R14,700 for a job instead of R15,000. We are not 100% certain why this is the case, it is just the way the
human brain works….
Interestingly, the one area where this strategy does not seem to hold sway is at the premium/luxury goods level. Research
shows that at this level customers respond more to rounded-off prices such as R1000 or R5000…
2.1.6 BE WISE ABOUT RAISING YOUR PRICES
It’s a fact of life that you will have to raise prices from time to time. If you never raise your prices, you won’t stay in
business for long. You have to constantly monitor your prices and your costs so that you are competitive in the market,
and that you make the kind of money you need to make.
That said, there are risks to raising prices, particularly when your customers themselves are going through tough financial
times. Recent food and petrol hikes have everyone twitchy about everything going up, so take into account the following
when you have to raise your prices:
Some Tips around Raising Prices:
• Do raise prices when your competitors are raising prices. If the competition has upped the ante, that is a good
signal that the market can and will support a price increase for your services, too.
• Do raise prices if your customers say you’re a bargain. If your customers keep commenting about what a great
value your prices are, that may be an indication you’re charging too little…
• Do raise prices if you introduce a new product or service. But only if you need to, not just for the sake of it.
• Don’t raise prices too much all at once. In a tough economy, a big jump in prices might be too much of a jolt for
your customers. Instead, raise prices in small increments of two or three price increases over the course of a year.
• Don’t raise prices across the board. Customers may not notice price increases if they are only for certain
products or services and not for others.
A new strategy that a lot of retailers are pursuing is Bundling, whereby they offer a better price if you buy three of an item
as an example. This is a strategy that can sometimes offset rising costs by driving volumes.

3.1 PRICING LEVELS EXPLAINED
We have spoken about pricing methodologies and the various information you need to consider when determining your
selling price. However, it is very seldom that you will sell to one type of customer only, so you will need to set different
levels of pricing to service the different customer types you may engage with.
Can you think of a few of the different types of customers you may encounter, and how their needs may differ from each other?

Remember, a key secret to being successful in sales is to make it as easy as possible for your client to do business with
you – and this starts with having a clear pricing strategy. As your business grows into new markets this can become quite
complicated, and so a clear understanding of some basics is essential if you are to be a confident and successful salesperson.
If you sell via different channels, you need to ensure you have the necessary pricing in place to service all of them. These
channels could include one or more of the following:
• Distributor pricing
• Wholesale pricing
• Retail pricing
• Corporate/Custom pricing
• Agent pricing
We are going to look at these various levels, explain how they differ and see how and where they fit in to your sales
strategy:
1. Distributor Pricing would be the lowest you are prepared to go, and reserved for your very largest customers, those
who order in volume and sell on to other shops and retailers in their markets. In order to qualify for this very low
pricing, distributors should:
• Be obliged to order in bulk (e.g. 200 of an item, minimum order value of R50,000)
• Sell on to other retailers, and be able to provide proof of their retail network
• Have set performance targets per quarter/year to maintain that pricing
• Have a contractual agreement with you highlighting all roles & responsibilities on both sides

2. Wholesale Pricing is the price charged by a manufacturer/distributor when selling products to a retailer, or by a
service-based business when selling bulk services to a middleman – such as bed nights at a guesthouse to a travel
agency, construction services to an architect or training for delegates to a conference organiser.
As with Distributor pricing, Wholesale pricing can have some conditions attached from your side as the supplier, e.g:
• Only customers buying for the purpose of resale can get wholesale prices
• Wholesale customers are obliged to stick to set sales terms & conditions and minimum quantities per product to
qualify for wholesale pricing
• You may ask a potential customer to provide proof of their credentials (e.g. where is the shop, other suppliers
they deal with, references etc)
3. Retail Pricing is when you sell directly to the end user. It should be pitched high (on a par with what other shops sell
your products for). This is important because if the retail outlets you supply at a Wholesale price (and who have to
buy in quantity to get that price) find out you are selling to the general public at the same price, they will no longer
support you.
Retail pricing is what you would charge when engaging in any form of direct selling, whether at a market, trade
show, via a website/e-commerce or shop/workspace. It would also apply when selling a service directly to the end
user, such as a game drive to a tourist who books directly or a training course directly to a delegate.
4. Corporate/Custom Pricing would involve prices quoted when the intention of the buyer is to use the items for
promotional purposes i.e. not to mark up and resell, or when you are designing or creating a special, customized
product or service for a certain client, such as a branded item with their company logo on it or a bespoke team-
building session that matches the company culture & focus.
This Corporate price will usually be pitched somewhere between your wholesale and retail price, both to cover the
development costs associated with customized work, and also because in most instances the buyer does not need to
make a profit from these products or services and has a different agenda. In a service context, this pricing would
apply when you customize a service especially for a client i.e. it is outside of your standard offerings.
Note that if you are creating a once-off, bespoke offering, whether product or service, you may actually need to
charge even more than you would for retail to cover your development time – depends on context.
5. Agent Pricing would be pricing you would give to a prospective agent, someone who will represent you in a certain
market or geographical area and sell on a commission basis. As a benchmark, your wholesale price plus 15%
commission would be an appropriate Agent price – the agent would then quote this price to the customer, who would
pay you directly and you would then pay over the 15% commission to the agent at an agreed date.
If you follow the guidelines above, do your research and talk to your customers, you will be empowered and feel confident
that the price you quote, is the correct one.

4. LESSON FOUR:
4.1 DEALING WITH EXPORT CUSTOMERS
The export market can seem daunting to new entrants, but remember it is a market like any other, and your main priority
is to ensure that you service your customers professionally and get the price you need to sustain your business.
It may be tempting to try and charge a higher price for export than for local customers; however, you must remember that
the costs of getting your products into an international market are much higher than they would be domestically – these
include things like freight, insurance, import duties and handling fees. Often you may need to charge even less than for
local sales! Also, you are suddenly competing against the rest of the world – if you are not competitive price-wise, or so
original and unique as to stand out, you are in for a tough time.
For this reason, our advice is to have a consistent level of pricing for local or export (whether it is wholesale, distributor
or retail) and be sure to add on separately any additional export-related charges such as document preparation, export
certificates, freight etc.
You might also consider higher minimum quantities per style and a higher minimum order value for export. This ensures
that it is worth your while to process an export order, which can involve a fair bit of paperwork, risk and time.
4.2 QUOTING IN FOREIGN CURRENCIES
When doing an international trade show, or even sometimes when negotiating with a foreign buyer who is interested in
your products/services, you may be expected to prepare a pricelist or quote in dollars, Euros or pounds.
This is an area that can cause a lot of uncertainty and stress given the volatility of the Rand and the potential to make
some very expensive mistakes. Remember, you are not in the business of currency speculation or gambling. If a certain
customer insists on you quoting in dollars or Euros, make sure you build in more than enough FAT to cover any fluctuations.
The same applies if you are developing pricelists for an international trade show. Always cover yourself, and always state
the conversion rate used to arrive at an international currency figure.

4.3 PROTECTING YOURSELF FROM FLUCTUATIONS
The most effective means of avoiding the dangers of forex fluctuation is to simply insist on always quoting in Rands,
however this is not always possible and sometimes you will have to quote in a foreign currency.
An effective way of managing risk is to build in some ‘fat’ so that should exchange rates go up or down, you remain
relatively well protected. As an example:
Current Spot Exchange Rate R/US$ = R19
Therefore, if you charge R100 in Rands for a product wholesale, you would ask $5.26
(R100 ÷ 19) in order to get your R100 for the item.
However, this conversion leaves you no room at all for fluctuation – if the Rand suddenly strengthened to R17.00 to the
dollar (unlikely yes), look at what would happen:
$5.26 x R17.00 = R89.42 – meaning you get R10.58 less per product than you wanted.
Now imagine that this happened on an order of 1000 items:
R10.58 x 1000 = a loss of R10580.00!!!
____________________________________________________________
If you had used a more favourable rate for yourself up front, and thus asked for slightly more dollars wholesale per piece,
you could have avoided this situation:
E.g. R100 ÷ R17.50 = $5.71 (and maybe round off If needed)
Remember to always state the rate of exchange used (some people even have a separate column in their pricelists
to indicate this), and also to be careful of pricing yourself out of the market by being too conservative and building
in too much fat.
Also, remember to always include in your sales terms & conditions that any prices quoted in forex are only guaranteed
for a limited period (usually 3 months), and that your company reserves the right to amend the pricing based on
excessive forex fluctuations. You can also consider Forward Cover as an additional measure.
4.4 DIFFERENT PRICES FOR DIFFERENT MARKETS
Some people believe that they should have different prices for different markets, such as local pricing vs. export pricing.
They may even take it a step further and have different prices for different regions e.g. EU prices, Scandinavian prices etc.
Our opinion is that this is a dangerous and frequently counter-productive strategy. Unless you are a truly global business
with the capacity and expertise to handle multiple different pricing strategies and account for geographical and regional
quirks and preferences, you may well end up getting into hot water by trying to carve up your pricing strategy into little bits.
Not only that, but it can also get confusing & administratively burdensome. Your priority as an entrepreneur is to ensure that:
1. You get the price you need for the item and
2. Your product arrives in the chosen market at a price that is competitive, fair to all parties and as consistent as
possible, so that it can sell.

5. LESSON FIVE:
5.1 DISCOUNTS
An Achilles heel of the SME and SMME sector – too often we create a climate where customers EXPECT a discount,
because we are so quick to offer one.
The crux – discounting must be strategic and must also be a win-win arrangement – I give, you give.
The Effects of Discounting
The effects of discounting can be quite dramatic, and very unexpected. For instance if you were selling a product with a
gross profit of 30% and, in order to increase customer sales or beat the competition, you decided to offer a discount of
10%, how many extra customers (as a percentage) do you think you would need to make the same profit overall?
The answer is at least 50% more customers!!
Did you guess correctly - a bit of bad luck if you didn’t? Unfortunately, you can’t afford bad luck in business. In fact, you
may be better off putting the price up slightly, losing some of your core customers and making the same profit (after all,
that’s what you’re in business to do, isn’t it?)
Many new businesses assume that if they sell at less than the competition, they will take their customers away. This is
rarely true and increasing your price, or charging the same but offering a bit more, is often a more sustainable way of
competing profitably.
Consider the following when considering a discounting strategy:
• Discounting should always be seen as a two-way street & negotiating tool
• Can the competition discount for longer than you?
• Will your core customers increase dramatically if you discount? You may be well advised to charge the same and
rather offer a higher level of service
• If you increased your price by just 5% you could afford to lose 15% of your customers and still make the same profit!
When is it Appropriate to offer a Discount?
Of course, there are certain scenarios and strategies where offering a discount is a reasonable and acceptable business
decision. These include:
• Quantity Discounting, where the order will be significantly increased as a result of the discount being offered.
However, do not offer a quantity discount off the bat, use it as a negotiating tool. Similarly, if it costs you exactly the
same to produce one or one hundred of an item, you need to think before offering a quantity discount as it is your
direct profit being discounted.
• Where a retailer is working according to set price points. Woolworths, Mr Price etc. will TELL YOU what they want
to pay for an item and you need to comply. In this instance, you are well within your rights to look at simplifying or
redesigning the product or service so that it is a win-win situation for all parties – we call this product engineering.

• In instances where the customer has been inconvenienced or otherwise prejudiced as a direct result of negligence,

• In a situation where you a-re trying to penetrate or break into a particular market and price is the only barrier

5.2 SOME MORE THOUGHTS ON PRICING
What if your price is not competitive?
If you have done your market and competitor research and find that your prices are unrealistic, the following should be
considered:
• Can you work more effectively and efficiently, by developing/improving your admin or production systems, or by
simplifying your processes?
• Can you decrease your overheads or running costs?
• Are there other potential income streams that could contribute towards your overheads or running costs?
• Can you focus on a niche market that would be willing to pay more?
• Can you add value without adding cost, as in offering a faster turnaround time? For such an offering to have a
positive impact, it is important that the customer knows about it and values it.
• Can you re-engineer your product or service and make it more competitive?
Can you think of any other strategies that you can try to make your prices work when they are too high based on your
costing & required profit levels?

Factors that can affect your pricing strategy and the price you are able to charge for your product
or service:
Apart from the materials used or the labour hours that go into the delivery of a product or service, there are several factors
that can also impact your pricing strategy:
Company Profile
The higher your company profile, experience and brand recognition, the more you can charge.
Objectives
Are your objectives to penetrate a market, beat the competition, establish yourself as a luxury brand? These will affect
your pricing significantly.
Client dynamics
If you really want to work with a certain client (such as a big corporate or well-known brand) you might go lower in price;
conversely, if you are expecting a difficult project or a slower payer, then you might want to increase your price.
Expect negotiation
People often add 10% to their initial quoted price so that they can give a discount or negotiate down, as some clients
might expect this. The jury is out on this, it depends on the client.
Product/service Engineering
Sometimes, your client gives a price that they are willing to pay and you let them know what you can do for that fee. This
may entail simplifying your product or service to come into price.
Job satisfaction
Sometimes we take on work that is not the most profitable, but provides excellent experience and exposure to new
thinking. Other times, we take on profitable work that is mind-numbingly boring, soul-destroying or even dangerous.
Always weigh up the pros and cons.
Pricing Services
In some organizations (such as a repair shop or a hairdresser), a consistent hourly rate will be charged regardless of
which employee actually works on the job; in other organizations, the rate may vary by employee. For example, in
an accounting firm, the rate charged for a junior accountant’s time will generally be less than the rate charged for an
experienced senior accountant, or for a partner.
There are a number of different ways of setting prices in a service environment, and it may be useful to quickly look at
some of them here:
Per hour – you determine an hourly rate and charge according to number of hours a given service takes to deliver. You
can set different hourly rates for different tasks, different rates based on who does the work, as well as the complexity of
the work.
Per job – you set a fixed price for a certain job, e.g. R500 per game drive, R150 per airport transfer, R2500 per
workshop.

Tips on Pricing Services
1. Test Your Prices with Existing Audiences
“Anytime we roll out a new product or service, we always test the price with a few — we usually limit the number of lists
to three — different existing segmented email lists that signed for that specific topic or a related offer to determine the
best pricing model.” ~ Kristin Kimberly Marquet, Creative Development Agency, LLC
2. Let the Market Price Your Product
“Pricing can be such a sore spot for many entrepreneurs, but it shouldn’t be. Few realize that the value is in the eye of the
buyer, which means you’ll always make the most money leaving the price up to the customer. Go have conversations —
test prices, ask whether buyers would buy within a range of prices, and explore why they would or wouldn’t. These easy
conversations will make you money.” ~ Peter Kozodoy, GEM Advertising
3. Start High
“It’s easier to come down in price than to go up. So your first step should be to generate a solid ballpark price. You can
look at similar products or services to do that, then do some testing or polling. Then I would recommend to start on the
high end of that ballpark or even a bit above it. If you need to have a sale later on, customers will appreciate that.” ~
Nicolas Gremion, Free-eBooks.net
4. Do Competitive Research
“For us, it all boils down to research. Learn what the market is paying for your new service and learn what your
competitors are charging for that service. Once you have that data, you can uniquely position yourself based on your
price point, quality and offering.” ~ Joel Mathew, Fortress Consulting
5. Figure Out Your Market Positioning
“The most important thing when you price for a service, tool or product is how you want to position yourself in the market.
Once you determine that, consider the price your competitors are charging for it and the number of expected achievable
customers. Then, ask if are you adding a value compared to competitors and if you can diversify pricing by creating
packages.” ~ Michael Hsu, DeepSky
6. Start At Twice Your Costs
“Start with a basic model work backward. First, figure out your costs, then aim at a price point of 2C, or twice your costs.
Later, review the marketspace to see what competitive offers and then adjust accordingly. Hopefully, your operation is
tight enough that you will be able to compete on value.” ~ Nicole Munoz, Nicole Munoz Consulting, Inc.
7. Conduct A/B Tests
“For us, we’ve found that continuous A/B testing is the best way to continue increasing conversions on our pricing page.
Experiment with a 14-day free trial, different packages, and monthly pricing versus yearly pricing. For example, if churn
is an issue for your business, a yearly price could help reduce your attrition rate, but you’ll only find out through testing.”
~ Syed Balkhi, WPBeginner

6.1 TERMS AND CONDITIONS OF SALES
Your terms & conditions of sale set out the parameters and requirements under which your business will engage in
transactions with potential or existing customers.
They should be seen as a binding and legal agreement between yourself and the customer in terms of your engagement
and interaction. Thus, they need to be communicated and agreed to clearly and up front before any work commences.
They should include:
• The prices of the goods or the services that have been purchased.
• The mode of payment for the goods or services purchased.
• Details and feature specifications of the goods or services.
• The details about the delivery date for the goods or services.
• The responsibilities of the customer according to the terms and conditions as decided upon by the seller and the
customer.
• The responsibilities of the supplier as decided upon by the seller and the customer.
• The limit on the liabilities of the supplier, in case of any problem.
• The details regarding the schedule of the proposal.
• In case the deal is between a business and a client (B2C), then details about the clause regarding cancellation of the
purchase order and refund of money needs to be included.
• In case of a deal between two business parties (B2B), there will be clauses regarding alterations, warranty,
compensation, intellectual property rights, and termination of the deal.

7.1 CREATING EFFECTIVE PRICELISTS & SALES PROPOSALS
Remember we said at the outset that successful selling is about making it as easy as possible to do business with you.
This starts with having a clear, concise and attractive pricelist or proposal that not only gives your potential customer all
the information they need to make a decision as to whether to buy or not, but also encourages them to do so.
What makes an effective Price List, Proposal or Catalog?
1. It’s clear, comprehensive and easy to use
Your price list, fee structure or catalog may very well be the most widely used document you produce. It’s a constant
source of reference for your customers. A well-designed, easy-to-use document makes it easier for them to do business with
you. It also illustrates your professionalism.
2. It complements your other marketing efforts
A good price list or catalog is a single source for all your product/service information. It should complement your other
marketing efforts, such as websites, brochures etc.
3. It makes the buying decision easy
It contains all the information buyers need and it makes it easy for them to find that information. Good layout, effective
descriptions, codes, quick references, relevant images and graphics and sales terms & conditions help to avoid user
frustration.
4. It’s cost-effective
Good design also contributes to lower production costs by making efficient use of space and keeping page counts to a
minimum. Save those trees!
5. It’s available in a variety of media
While there will always be a need for some hard copy price lists, the era of digital pricing and product information has
arrived. You need to be able to distribute this data on CDs, DVDs, by e-mail broadcast, or over the Web.
Basic Structure
The style of pricelists may differ markedly, but certain key elements are common in most of them:
Intro – some background information on your company, philosophy, products, awards & accolades, services and offerings
etc. Keep it concise (maximum one page). It is often a good idea to include an image or two.
Main Body – your product and pricing information including code, price, minimum quantities, colours and a reference
picture if possible for products, and full breakdowns of your training, workshops and other offerings if you sell services.
Sales Terms & Conditions – all your terms and conditions of sale including payment terms, freight & delivery etc.

3. Next click Footer, and fill in your contact details
