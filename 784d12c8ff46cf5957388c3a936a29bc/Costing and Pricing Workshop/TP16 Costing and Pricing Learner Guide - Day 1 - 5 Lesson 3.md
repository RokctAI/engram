DIRECT LABOUR COSTS (DIRECT COST)

When calculating the cost of your Direct Labour for a certain product, contract or job, in other words labour that you must
pay for in terms of time or money to complete a certain order or deliver a particular service, there are several things to
remember:

•  You need to know beforehand how long it will take to complete a certain product or deliver a certain service (or even
a tender or a contract) before you can arrive at an accurate labour costing. This applies whether you pay by the
hour, by the job or per contract, and whether you make/deliver the product or service yourself or pay multiple other
staff or service providers to do so.

•

If you pay your service providers, employees or subcontractors per job, you will still need to have determined an
hourly rate that both you and the subcontractor are happy with before production/implementation begins.  The actual
payment per product or service delivered will usually then be drawn from how long that product or service reasonably
takes to do x the agreed hourly rate.

•  You can pay/allocate different labour rates for different skills, according to their level of difficulty and/or availability

or scarcity.

Examples:

If a training workshop takes 4 hours and the agreed hourly rate with the trainer is R250/hr, he will be due R250 x 4 =
R1000 upon completion.

If paying a contracted seamstress per item and a garment takes 2 hours to sew from start to finish, and the agreed rate for
that type of product or skill is R100/hour, then R200 would be a fair price to pay per completed item. If a highly-skilled
producer can make the item in 1.5 hours, they should still be paid R200 – you need to set the rate at an average skill
level and not at the highest or lowest…

•

•

In a manufacturing/production set-up it is usually more efficient and cost effective to use different producers for stages
of the production process, instead of having one producer make the whole item. The time of each stage will need to
be known or at the very least accurately estimated if you are to ensure accurate costings, and also if you need to pay
different producers for their particular contribution along the production line.

In  many  cases,  it  is  wise  to  work  out  a  minute  rate  for  each  producer/employee,  especially  if  a  product  involves
several small steps in the production process. This is how much it costs the company, per minute, for that employee’s
time. Services, by contrast will usually be costed on an hourly, daily or even weekly basis.

To work out a minute rate, start with the agreed daily wage and then work backwards as follows:

•  Agreed Daily Rate = R500

R500 ÷ 7hrs = R71.43 per hour  (avg 8-hour workday with an hour for breaks deducted)

R71.43 ÷ 60 minutes = R1.19c per minute

(This is the employee’s minute rate)

CALCULATING DIRECT LABOUR RATES FOR SALARIED EMPLOYEES

Direct  manufacturing  labour  costs  are  the  total  costs  of  staff  who  work  directly  on  goods  being  manufactured.  It  is  a
useful measurement for a small business to use because it allows you to understand exactly how much of your wage bill
is going toward employees who actually manufacture your products. Calculate this cost by identifying direct labourers
and computing their wages:

1.  Determine which workers are considered direct labourers. Direct labourers are workers who directly perform work
on the product being produced. For example, in a bakery the person who mixes the dough or bakes the bread is a
direct labourer, but the person who sweeps the floors is not…

2.  Add up the total hours that all direct labourers work in the period in question (per month, per quarter or per year).
If you had four direct labourers who worked 400, 300, 200 and 500 hours, you would add these hours up to get
1,400 hours.

3.  Multiply the number of hours worked over the period by their labour rate. This will give you the total direct labour
costs. Assuming that your wage rate is R50 per hour, you would multiply it by 1,400 -- the number of hours in this
example  --  to  get  total  direct  manufacturing  costs  of  R70  000  for  the  period.  You  could  then  calculate  this  as  a
percentage of your total wage bill and get a ratio of direct vs indirect labour in your company.

DIRECT LABOUR COSTING – THINGS TO REMEMBER:

•

If you manufacture or deal in any goods that need to be packed, you need to remember to cost in any labour you pay
for in terms of packing and packaging of products – this is a cost centre that many ignore or forget about.

•  Make sure you are familiar with your rights & obligations as an employer, both to protect yourself in case of any

problems, and also to ensure your employees are pulling their weight!

•

If you make use of subcontractors for all or part of your manufacturing or service provision process, the terms and
amounts of payment must be agreed up front in writing and quality standards, deadlines, policies in terms of reject
products or poor service delivery must be discussed and agreed upon as well. Have a contract or at least an SLA
(Service Level Agreement) in place.

•  You need to ensure that your organisation makes a profit from the money it spends on labour.

•

If you pay people per day or per week, be sure that they stick to the times that have been allowed for production in the
costing – this usually entails setting tangible production targets and ensuring that these are followed. You may even
want to use incentives to encourage productivity. This also applies to the delivery of a service – if you have allowed
for 5hrs to do a certain piece of work or perform a certain function, you need to ensure it is completed within that
time frame....

•  Costs  like  weekly  or  monthly  salaries  and  wages  paid  to  staff  members  who  are  not  directly  involved  in  physical
production or actual delivery of services must be costed as overheads and not as direct labour. This includes marketing
functions, admin, sales, accounts etc.
