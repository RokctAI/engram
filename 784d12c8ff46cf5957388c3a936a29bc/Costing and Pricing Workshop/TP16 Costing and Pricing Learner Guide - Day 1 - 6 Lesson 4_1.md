COSTING IN THE AGRICULTURE SECTOR
Costing in Agriculture follows similar rules to any other sector, but it does come with a couple of differences, which makes
it an interesting case study. Below we discuss four main ones:
1. Length of the production cycle can extend across many months, or even years
2. Relationship between inputs and outputs is not fixed, and is influenced by factors out of the farmer’s control (eg
temperature, day length, rainfall)
3. High capital investment costs for land and equipment require repayment over an extended period
4. Fixed pricing and market monopolies limit pricing options for certain produce.
________________________________________________________________________________________________________
1. Lengthy Production Cycles. Certain crops, for example Macadamia nuts, cattle and oranges only begin
bearing fruit many months and even years into the cycle. The longer the gap between the cost of starting the farm
(planting the trees or buying the cows) the greater the need for capital to cover cashflow deficit. Careful planning
and budgeting is needed to ensure the farm doesn’t run out of money before the harvest happens and income starts
to flow into the business.
2. Variable Input: output relationship. Unlike simple manufacturing costing where one bracelet always uses 5
beads, the outputs in farming (e.g. chicken weight, time to harvest and yield) are determined not only by the inputs
(fertiliser, seeds, water, food) but by other factors out of the farmer’s control. This is one of the reasons why farming
is such a tricky industry to be in, and the reason why farmers invest in irrigation, lighting and greenhouses – to try
and control these other variables. The lack of a fixed input: output relationship also means that whilst costing can be
forecast using a historical budget (ie. what results you got in the past) it also needs to be costed on a real-time basis
- to check your actual results and price each time and with every harvest.
3. High Capital Cost. Farming is notorious for its high capital cost – especially if you need to purchase land at
commercial prices. In addition, the cost of buildings, irrigation equipment and tractors adds a huge price to the
production cost. It is important that these are included in the costing models – but divided over a realistic period.
Land for example is usually repaid over 25 years, tractors and buildings over 10 years and other equipment over a
shorter period. Even if your land and equipment has been paid for already, your costing must build in the price of
replacing this equipment, or you will have no funds to replace the buildings and machinery when they become old.
4. Fixed pricing in agriculture is a challenge that often requires the farmer to reduce their cost of production in
order to make a profit. The dairy industry is notorious for this worldwide, with the market price for milk fixed below
breakeven in many first world countries – requiring farmers to exist on subsidies. Locally this impacts the small farmer
who is dictated to by the market, which is much bigger and stronger than he/she is. Strategies for better pricing
include producing ‘out of season’ when fewer people are selling and prices are higher, doing value-adds such as
packaging and processing, and producing special, quality or unusual products – for example black tomatoes, green
cauliflower and red runner beans.

COSTING BASICS – SEASONAL FINANCE: INTEREST
Most commercial farmers have to present a production budget to the bank every year with a request for seasonal finance. This
budget includes an estimate of the inputs (seed, fertiliser, electricity etc) and the projected yield and price showing income.
The bank manager needs to be convinced that that the farmer is a ‘good risk’ and there is a good chance that he will
repay the loan once the harvest has been made.
Seasonal finance will be given to the farmer to purchase the inputs, pay labour, pay electricity and all other costs for the
entire growing season, and interest is calculated on the total overdraft on a monthly basis.
These interest costs need to be included in your costing, to ensure that you make a profit at the end. Challenges arise
when a crop sale is delayed or when yields and/or prices are lower than expected, as bank managers generally just
want their money on time!

COSTING BASICS: CALCULATING THE PRICE OF ONE BROILER CHICKEN
Many farmers are really great at recording their total expenses, but struggle to work out how much one cabbage, one
chicken or a kilogram of beans actually costs to produce.
The fundamentals are exactly the same as all costing models – namely that one must understand how to calculate the
rating (how much is used in the production) and price per single unit (kg, litre) of input.
In broiler production, if we know how much feed etc goes into the chicken house, and we know how many chickens are
in the house, it is possible to work out the cost per chicken quite easily.

We calculate how much he must put aside (save) each month to ensure there are funds to replace the old buildings and

5.   Total direct costs plus overhead cost per chicken
•  To work out the Total Cost, we add the Direct and Overhead costs for the house (R67,640 + 13152 = R80,792)
•  To calculate cost per chicken we divide our Total Cost by the number of chickens sold (2700).* Remember to factor
in the mortality rate (losses) – in this case we started with 3000 chicks and estimated a 10% loss rate (3000 - 300

The total cost per chicken is therefore total costs divided by number sold = (R80,792/2700 = R30)
This gives us a cost per chicken, factoring in all our costs (direct, overhead etc) of R30 each
